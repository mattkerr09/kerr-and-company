A church website has two readers. A visitor wants to know when you meet, where, what to expect and who to ask. A member wants this week's message, the calendar and the giving link. Neither is complicated. Church sites go wrong by being out of date, by hiding the service times in a PDF, and by being built by a volunteer who has since moved away. What a church site needs, and where each thing goes

The first screen answers when and where

Someone new to the area, or new to church, is deciding whether to come on Sunday. The first thing they see should be the service times and the address, in text a phone can read aloud and a search engine can index. A page that opens with a slogan and a full-screen photograph, with the times three scrolls down, loses the person the site exists for.

A "new here" page

How long the service runs, what happens with children, what people wear, whether there is coffee, and what to expect if you have never been. This is the page that turns a search into a visit, and it is the one most church sites do not have. On the Starter package it is usually the second of the three pages.

Messages, embedded rather than hosted

If your messages are already on a video platform or a podcast host, the site embeds them from there. That keeps the site fast, keeps the church's existing audience where it is, and means the newest message appears without anyone touching the site. Video hosting is a third-party service and its fees, if any, are the church's; we do not host or edit video.

Giving and events, kept current

A giving link belongs in the header and on its own short page that says what the money goes to. Events need an owner: a site that lists a Christmas Eve time from two years ago is worse than one that lists nothing, because the reader no longer trusts anything on it. We say below who updates what.

Ministries, groups and people

Small groups, youth, music, outreach, and the staff and leadership with a face and a name. These are what push a church from three pages to five or eight, which is the line between the two packages.

Readable by everyone in the room

Congregations include people who read on a phone at arm's length and people who navigate with a keyboard. Readable text sizes, contrast that holds up, and tap targets big enough for a thumb are part of how we build, and the accessibility page lists exactly which of those we test on the pages we publish, and what we cannot promise.

Giving and donation tools: an honest note

Online giving runs through a provider the church chooses — a giving platform or a payment processor with its own account, its own fees, and its own receipts and year-end statements. We do not process donations, hold funds, or sit anywhere in that flow. What we build is the connection: a link, a button, or an embedded form the provider supplies, placed where members will find it and tested so it opens the right thing.

Two practical consequences. First, the provider's subscription or transaction fees are the church's, the same as any paid app you ask us to connect; they are not part of our price and we will not pretend they are. Second, the choice of provider is the church's to make, with its own treasurer and its own bank, and we will say plainly when a question about fees, receipts or tax statements is one for the provider rather than for a web designer.

Committees, volunteers, and why church projects stall
