What each trade needs from its site

The bones are the same for every trade — a clear offer, a form, a number, and pages that answer the questions people search — but the emphasis moves.

Roofers

Storm and hail damage drives the urgent calls, so the home page leads with it and says plainly whether you handle the insurance claim. A before-and-after gallery does more than any paragraph, which means your own job photos matter here more than in any other trade. Photography is not included in the price; you supply the photos, or we use stock until you have them.

HVAC

Two seasons, two different visitors. In January the visitor has no heat and wants a number to call above the fold. In April the visitor is comparing a new system and wants to read. The site has to serve both: click-to-call first, then a page each for repair, replacement and maintenance so the planned work has somewhere to land.

Plumbers

The emergency path — a burst pipe, a backed-up drain — needs the phone number in the first screen and nothing in the way. The estimate form is for the planned jobs: a water heater, a bathroom rough-in, a sump pump. Separating those two paths on the page is most of the design.

Electricians

Panel upgrades, EV charger installation, generator hook-ups, whole-house rewiring: each is a distinct search with a distinct customer, and a page for each is how a site ranks for any of them. The licence and insurance line people look for belongs near the top, not in the footer.

Remodelers

The longest sales cycle of the six. Visitors want to see finished work and understand the process before they will talk, so the site is gallery-led and the ask is a scheduled walkthrough rather than a "free quote" form. There is more on why that wording matters below.

Landscapers and lawn care

Seasonal services — spring clean-ups, mowing, fall leaf removal, snow — and a business defined by a driving radius. Town pages matter most for this trade, because the question is almost always "do they come out to us".

The estimate form, and why the wording around it matters

Every contractor site offers a free estimate. It is the default because it feels frictionless. Our article Why "free estimate" loses to a calendar link makes the case against it: a form that costs the visitor nothing proves nothing about their intent, so somebody on your team ends up phoning thirty people to find the three who were serious. A booked time costs the visitor something real, and that cost is the qualification. The article includes rewritten offers for a roofer, an HVAC company and a landscaper, each naming a specific thing, a time, and an honest price.

The evidence behind it comes from a housing campaign, not a contractor one, and we say so. The $3.82 cost-per-lead case study produced 47 booked home-tour leads on $179 of ad spend by asking people to pick a house and a time rather than to "learn more". The case study's own conclusion is that the transferable part is the structure — a specific, high-intent offer, a frictionless way to say yes, and fast follow-up — and that the pattern works for estimates, quotes and appointments in most local service businesses. It also lists its caveats, including that a small pilot is not a scaled programme. What actually moves cost per lead is the arithmetic behind it.

For a contractor site that means the estimate form stays, but the offer around it gets specific: what you will do, when, and what it costs, including when the answer is nothing. Both pieces are worth ten minutes before we write your home page, because the offer is the thing we will write.

Will my site rank for "roofer near me"?

Every build includes on-page SEO, local schema and Google Business Profile setup, which is the groundwork. Where you rank depends on your reviews, your competition and how many towns and services you cover, and nobody can promise a position. If ranking is the goal, the local SEO page describes the ongoing work.

What if I have no photos?

You supply images or we use stock; photography is not included in the price. Job photos from your own phone are usually better than stock for a contractor, and the site can launch with stock and swap them in as you collect them.

Can I pay monthly?

On the Starter site, yes: $149 a month for twelve months, then the payments stop and the site is yours. You can buy out the remaining balance whenever you like. If you stop early the site comes down and we hold it for 90 days with the buy-out open. Business and Growth builds are half now, half at launch, negotiable on larger builds.
