What is different when we work remotely

The sessions happen on your screens

The advisory call, the assessment and the training are video calls with your screen shared. That is not a compromise; it is where the decision lives. Whether a tool is worth using, whether a workflow can be automated, whether a vendor's demo matches what the product does — those are all things on a screen, and a shared screen in Lansing looks exactly like a shared screen in Grand Rapids. Written recommendations follow every session, so nothing depends on memory.

We cannot walk your floor, so you show us

The opportunity assessment is a look at your operations — where time and leads leak out, what is worth automating and in what order. In person, some of that comes from watching. Remotely, it comes from you: a walk-through of the systems you use, the steps people take, and where work waits. Plan for the two weeks to include some of your team's time. The assessment is only as good as what we get to see, and we will say early if we are not seeing enough.

Training is live, remote or in person, and recorded

The team training workshop is a hands-on session on your team's real work and your own tools, not generic prompt tricks. It can be run in person or remotely; remotely, the session is recorded for the people who miss it, and your team keeps a written playbook either way. Whether an in-person day makes sense for a business in Detroit or Traverse City, and what it costs, is agreed in writing per project rather than guessed at here.

Nothing changes on scope or ownership

The fixed quote, the written scope, and the fact that the plan and the playbook are yours to keep do not depend on geography. Neither does the follow-through: if the plan says build, the building is also done remotely, and AI automation and custom web tools are priced on their own pages.

Where AI actually helps a Michigan business

The question is about operations, not geography. A plumber in Kalamazoo misses the same calls a plumber in Grand Rapids misses; a dental practice in Ann Arbor has the same review problem as one in Kentwood. The automations that make small businesses the most money are usually the boring ones: texting back every missed call, asking every customer for a review, getting a new lead to the right phone in seconds instead of hours, giving an instant quote while the person is still interested, and pulling the numbers into one screen. Most businesses do not need an AI strategy; they need two or three specific things automated and someone honest about which ones.

The assessment sorts your list. It reviews your operations and tooling, hands back a prioritised plan of what to automate, what to buy off the shelf, and what to ignore, with build-versus-buy recommendations and the return on each. It also covers the data question — what is safe to send to a cloud model and what should stay local — which comes up in every Michigan conversation and has a sane answer once you know what the data is.

If the thing you are considering is advertising rather than automation, the $3.82 cost-per-lead case study and what actually moves cost per lead show how we read a campaign, and ads management is a separate, flat-fee service.
