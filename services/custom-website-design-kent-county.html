What "custom" means here

Plenty of "custom" sites are a purchased theme with a logo dropped in. Mine are written from a blank page: your words, your offer, your one most important action, then a design built around getting a visitor to take it.

Written for you

Copy comes from a call about your business: what you sell, who calls, what a job is worth. Not from a placeholder that says "Lorem ipsum" or "quality service you can trust".

Built around one action

A plumber wants calls. A salon wants bookings. A manufacturer wants a request for quote. The whole page is arranged to make that action the obvious next step.

No template ceiling

When you want a feature the theme does not have, a template site stalls. A custom one gets the feature: an instant quote, an intake bot, a booking tool.

Yours outright

Domain, hosting and code sit in your name from day one. Move it, edit it, hand it to someone else. Nothing is locked to a builder subscription. Here is how to check who owns yours.

WordPress, a builder, or hand-built?

If you searched for a custom WordPress designer, here is the straight answer. For a new site I default to hand-written HTML, CSS and a little JavaScript on a content delivery network. There is no theme and no plugin waiting for a security patch, and I test every site with Google's Lighthouse before launch. If you already have a WordPress site that is doing its job, I work inside it instead of rebuilding it.

WordPress is the common choice around here. On 23 September 2026 I read the home pages of 266 working trade websites in seven West Michigan towns: 130 (49%) run self-hosted WordPress and 82 (31%) sit on a hosted builder such as Wix, Duda or Squarespace. The platform count has the method and the full table. Speed varies a lot too. In September I ran Google's Lighthouse test on ten Grand Rapids contractor home pages and the mobile scores ran from 31 to 99, with a median of 59. The speed article shows how I measured.
