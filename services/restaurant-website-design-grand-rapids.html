What a restaurant site needs, and why each part is built the way it is

The menu is text, not a PDF

A PDF menu is the most common mistake on restaurant sites and the most expensive one. It has to be downloaded, it is unreadable on a phone without pinching, it cannot be searched, and Google cannot read the dishes inside it, so a search for something you make finds nothing. It also goes out of date, because updating a PDF means opening a design file. On our builds the menu is text with the prices beside the dishes, and changing a price is changing a line. If you also want a printable version for the tables, it can be offered as a download alongside the text, not instead of it.

Hours, including the exceptions

Kitchen hours and bar hours if they differ, brunch if you do it, the holiday closures, the summer patio. Hours belong in text near the top of the first screen, not inside an image, and in the page's structured data so that Google's listing and your site never disagree. A restaurant site with wrong hours is worse than one with none, because the person who drove over is not coming back.

Location, parking, and the door

The address as a link that opens the map app, a line about parking, and, if the entrance is not obvious, which door. A phone number that dials with one tap. These are small things, and they decide whether a first-time visitor arrives.

Reservations and online ordering

Reservations, online ordering and delivery run through a service the restaurant chooses. We connect the site to it as a link or as the embedded widget the provider supplies, placed where a visitor will find it and tested so it opens the right thing. The provider is a third party: its subscription, its per-cover or per-order fees and its terms are between you and the provider, and they are not part of our price. A custom ordering or payment system is a different build, scoped on its own, and we will say so rather than squeeze it into a three-page site.

Photographs: yours

Photography and logo design are not included. You supply the photographs, or we use stock until you have them. Our article on why website projects stall argues that most businesses should launch without waiting for a photographer, and then makes an exception it names directly: restaurants, where the appearance of the food is the purchase decision and placeholders undersell you. Its advice is to launch the pages that do not depend on imagery and stage the visual ones. In practice the menu, hours and contact pages go live first, and the photographs come in as you get them. Swapping a photograph is a five-minute job on a site built this way.

The pages a larger restaurant adds

Private dining and events, catering, gift cards, a page for the people and the story, a press page. Each is its own page because each is a separate search and a separate customer. This is the line between the two packages below.

Google Business Profile: where most restaurant clicks come from

When someone searches for a restaurant near them, or for a kind of food plus "Grand Rapids", the businesses in the map pack are the ones that get the taps. That listing is your Google Business Profile, and it shows your hours, your photographs, your reviews, a menu link and a reservation link before the person has opened your site at all. The local SEO page explains what drives that ranking — the right category, completeness, review volume, consistent listings and activity — and that it is not how good the website looks.

Two consequences for the site. First, every build here includes Google setup: the profile is set up or corrected so it points at the site, and the hours, address and phone on the site match the ones on the profile. Inconsistent name, address and phone data across listings is one of the boring, fixable reasons local businesses lose search traffic. Second, reviews are the thing most likely to move the ranking, and the site cannot generate them. The local SEO page describes a review system that messages customers after a visit and routes happy ones to Google and unhappy ones to you privately first. That work is separate from the site: a one-time Profile Tune-Up is $750 and ongoing management starts at $500 a month per location, both on that page.
