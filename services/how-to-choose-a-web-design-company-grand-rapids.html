Choosing a Web Design Company · Grand Rapids, MI

How to choose among web design companies in Grand Rapids

The questions that separate a good quote from a bad one — who writes the copy, fixed price or hourly, who owns the code and the domain at the end, what happens if the project stalls — and this studio's own answer to each, including the things it does not do.

Search for web design companies in Grand Rapids and you get a page of names, a few published prices and a lot of "contact us for a quote". Price is a weak signal in both directions, and a portfolio screenshot tells you what a site looked like on launch day, not whether it brings in work. This page is the list of questions we would ask if we were hiring a web design company, what a good answer sounds like, what a fixed quote should contain, and how this studio answers each question — including the ones where the honest answer is "we do not do that".

Who writes the copy?

The one question that predicts whether the project ships. If the answer is "you do", the hardest part of the job is yours and it has no deadline.

Fixed price or hourly?

A fixed price in writing means the risk of a slow build is theirs. An hourly rate with an estimate means it is yours, and an estimate is not a price.

Who owns it at the end?

Code, content, domain and every login should be in your name. If any of them is not, leaving costs you the site.

What happens if it stalls?

Every project has a cancellation clause or it has none. Ask to see it before the deposit, not after.

The four questions, and what the answers tell you

Who writes the copy?

Almost no website project is late because the developer is slow. Our article on why website projects stall goes through where the months go on a typical small-business build: the design and the build are a few days of work, and the longest single stretch is the gap between a content request going out and the owner sending back photos, service descriptions and page copy. That request lands on the busiest person in the business, with no deadline and no template, and it loses to every task that has a customer attached.

So ask each company what it needs from you before it can start. The good answer is short: access to your domain, your existing site or listings, your services and prices, and a twenty-minute conversation. The bad answer is a content questionnaire, a photo package and written copy for every page. The second answer means the hard part is yours and there is no date attached to it.

Fixed price or hourly?

A fixed price and a date in writing before you commit puts the risk of a slow build on the builder. An hourly rate with an estimate puts it on you. Neither model is dishonest, but you should know which one you are signing. Ask for the total, the payment schedule, and what is quoted separately.

The published local rates are in what Grand Rapids web designers charge: most small-business sites in the city land between $3,000 and $15,000, local freelancers commonly quote $2,500 to $6,000 for a hand-built five-to-seven-page site, and maintenance runs $50 to $300 a month. The article's point is that the gap between an $8,000 site and a $999 site is mostly the number of people who have to be paid to talk to each other about it, not the quality of the code. A higher price usually means more people, and only two of them touch your website.

Who owns the code and the domain at the end?

The answer should be you, unambiguously, and it should be true from day one rather than after a final payment. Check four things: the domain is registered in your name, the hosting account is yours, you have the source code or can take it with you, and every login — analytics, the Google Business Profile, the form service — is yours. A company that hosts your site on its own account and registers the domain for you has built a site you cannot leave with. Ask what happens if you leave, and listen for a pause.

What happens if the project stalls?

Ask two things: what the cancellation terms are, and what the company does when a project sits in limbo. A written clause that says what you pay if work stops halfway is normal; the absence of one is not. And a company with a plan for a stalled project — drafting the missing content itself, launching with placeholder photographs and swapping them in later — will finish more projects than one that waits for you.

What a fixed quote should contain

A fixed quote is a document, not a number in an email. Before you pay a deposit to any web design company in Grand Rapids, it should state each of these, and you should be able to point to the line.

The pages, by name. "Up to five pages" is a count. "Home, services, about, gallery, contact" is a scope, and it is the only kind you can hold anyone to.

The price and the payment schedule. The total, when each part is due, and what is quoted separately.

The date, and what it depends on. A date that assumes you reply within a stated time is fair. A date with no assumptions stated is a guess.

Revision rounds. How many are included and what happens after them.

What is not included. Photography, logo, domain, third-party subscriptions, hosting after launch. A quote that lists none of these has not thought about them.

Ownership. Who owns what at the end, and in whose name the domain and hosting are registered.

The cancellation terms. In the agreement, not in a conversation.

How long a website should take suggests one more question for the timeline line: of those weeks, how many is the company working on your site? The production work on a five-to-seven-page service site is two to five working days. The rest of a long timeline is queue time and waiting, and you are entitled to know which part is which.

Red flags, and one thing that is not one

Not publishing prices is not a red flag on its own; scope genuinely varies and there are decent reasons to quote each job. These are red flags.

Hosting or a domain in the company's name, or a "platform" you cannot leave with your site intact.

A content questionnaire as step one, with no date on it and no offer to draft for you.

A maintenance plan that is required rather than offered.

A timeline with no breakdown of working days against calendar weeks.

A portfolio you cannot open. Screenshots are not a site. Open the live site on your phone, on cellular, and search the client's business name plus the city to see whether the site the company built ranks for its own business.

Reviews you cannot verify, on the company's site or on a client's.

"We will add tracking later." A form that lands in a spam folder and analytics that were never installed are both things you discover months in.

The pricing article also has a list it calls the floor: responsive on phones including the forms, fast on a cellular connection, a contact method you have tested, unique page titles and descriptions, structured data, a sitemap, SSL, analytics installed and reporting, and ownership of the domain, content and code. Anything missing from that list means the quote was incomplete, whatever it cost.

Questions people ask

How much does a website cost in Grand Rapids?

Published local rates put most Grand Rapids small-business sites between $3,000 and $15,000, and local freelancers at $2,500 to $6,000 for a hand-built five-to-seven-page site. Our prices are published: a Starter Site from $999, a Business Site from $2,499, a Growth Site with one custom tool from $4,999, and a Care Plan from $199 a month. Every project gets a fixed quote in writing before work begins.

Should I choose a freelancer or an agency?

Hire an agency when you have many stakeholders who need managing, when original photography and video are part of the deliverable, when you run e-commerce with real inventory complexity, when you need a guaranteed support level, or when you are migrating a large established site. Otherwise most of an agency price buys coordination between people, and a five-page site for a local business does not need much of it.

How do I check a web design company's work?

Run their own site through PageSpeed Insights. Open a site they built on your phone, on cellular, rather than a screenshot. Search a client's business name plus the city and see whether the site ranks for its own business. Ask who owns the code and the domain, and what happens if you leave. Ask what happens after launch and whether fixing a broken form in month four is billed.

What should a fixed website quote include?

The pages by name, the price and payment schedule, a date and what it depends on, the number of revision rounds, what is not included, who owns what at the end and in whose name the domain and hosting are registered, and the cancellation terms. Ask how many of the quoted weeks the company is actually working on your site.

Yes. The build happens over a call and a shared link, so the town changes the local search setup rather than the site. There are separate pages for Kent County and West Michigan, and the work is done remotely for anyone in the United States.
