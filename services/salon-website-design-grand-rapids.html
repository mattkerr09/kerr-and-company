A salon website has one job: turn the person who just found you into a booked appointment without a phone call. That means the two things a visitor wants are on the first screen — what you do and what it costs — and the booking is one tap away. Salon sites go wrong by hiding prices, by making booking a phone number that rings out while your hands are in someone's hair, and by being built on a platform the owner cannot log into. What a salon site needs, and how each part is built

The service menu, with prices

Publishing prices is the decision most salon owners hesitate over and the one that most affects bookings. A visitor who cannot find a price assumes the worst and books somewhere that shows one. Where the price depends on length, thickness or the work involved, a "from" price with a line explaining what moves it is honest, and it still answers the question. The menu is text on the page, grouped the way you group it — cut, colour, treatments, nails, lashes, waxing, men's — with durations, so the menu and the booking tool agree.

Online booking: two honest routes

Most salons already run booking software, and it is usually the right tool: it holds the calendar, each stylist's hours, the deposits and the reminders. On a build here we connect the site to it as a link or as the embedded widget the provider supplies, on every page and on every stylist's page. The software is a third party you choose. Its subscription and its card-processing fees are yours, not part of our price, and we say which product before you commit.

The other route is a custom booking tool built for you, which is what the demonstration shows and what the Growth Site + Tool package includes: a booking flow on your own site that writes to your calendar and sends a text confirmation. If that tool needs a paid service to work — a texting provider, a calendar product — that subscription is yours, and we say which one before you commit. It costs more than connecting the software you have, and for most salons connecting the software you have is the right answer. We will say which you are on the first call rather than sell the tool.

A page for each stylist

A name, a photograph, the services they take, the days they work and their own booking link. In a commission salon it lets a client find their person. In a chair-rental salon each stylist is a business, and a page each is how a search for their name lands on your site rather than on a social profile. Stylist pages, plus a page per service category and a gallery, are what move a salon from three pages to five or eight, which is the line between the two packages below.

Photographs

Photography is not included; you supply the photographs, or we use stock until you have them. Our article on why website projects stall tells most businesses to launch without waiting on a photographer and then names salons as the exception: when the product's appearance is the purchase decision, placeholders undersell you. The practical version is to launch the service menu, the booking and the contact pages now, and bring the gallery in as the work gets photographed. A phone photograph of finished hair in good light does more than any stock image.

Policies, in plain words

Cancellation window, deposits, late arrivals, children, what to bring to a first appointment. They are your policies; we write them clearly and put them where a person looks before they book, because a policy someone finds afterwards is an argument.

Business Profile and reviews

When someone searches "salon near me" or "barber Grand Rapids", the three businesses in the map pack get the taps. That listing is your Google Business Profile, and it shows your hours, photographs, reviews and booking link before your site has loaded. The local SEO page explains that the ranking is driven mostly by the profile itself — the right category, completeness, review volume, consistent listings, activity — and not by how good the website looks.

Every build here includes Google setup, so the profile points at the site and the hours, address and phone match everywhere. Reviews are the other half, and for a salon they are the deciding one: a person choosing who will cut their hair reads them. The local SEO page describes a review system that messages a client after their appointment and routes happy ones to Google, with unhappy ones going to you privately first. That is separate from the site: a one-time Profile Tune-Up is $750, and ongoing management starts at $500 a month per location.

Barbers, spas, lash and nail studios: what changes

The bones are the same for every appointment business — a priced menu, a booking path, a number, and pages that answer the questions people search — but the emphasis moves.

Barbershops. Walk-ins or booked, the hours and the number above everything else. The service list is short and the prices are round. A page per barber if they rent chairs.

Day spas and medical spas. Longer service descriptions, packages, and gift cards through a third-party provider. For a medical spa, treatment claims are regulated copy that needs a specialist to sign off, and we say so up front rather than write around it.

Lash and nail studios. The gallery is the sale, so the photographs matter most here. Full-set and fill pricing side by side, and a booking flow that asks the two questions you would ask on the phone.

Hair salons. Colour pricing that depends on the work, so "from" prices with a consultation offered, and a page per stylist. If some of your clients read on a phone at arm's length, the accessibility page lists what we test for text size, contrast and tap targets.
