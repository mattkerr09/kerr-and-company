What should a painting company website have? I checked 48 West Michigan painters: only 31 listings lead to a live website, and none of the 30 I could read shows a price

On 23 September 2026 the first page of results for “what should a painting company website have” was ten how-to guides and design showcases, most of them from companies that sell software, hosting, themes or web design — written for painters anywhere, measured nowhere. I wanted the local answer: on the websites of the painters a search actually shows in West Michigan, what is standard, and where is the gap a painter could own? So I took the local listings for painters in eight towns and followed each one as far as it goes.

By Matthew Kerr Published 23 Sep 2026 Updated 23 Sep 2026 14 min read
Quick answer

Start with the listing, not the website. On 23 September 2026 I checked the 48 painters Brave Search lists for Wyoming, Kentwood, Grandville, Holland, Muskegon and Kalamazoo (Grand Rapids and Walker returned none). Only 31 of the 48 listings lead to a live website: 10 name none, 2 link a Facebook or Instagram page instead, and 5 name a site that is dead or parked. Before anything else, a painting company website has to be where your listings point, and it has to load.

One of those 31 is drawn entirely by script, so its HTML carries no content for a single fetch to read. The other 30 have a common baseline — all on HTTPS with a phone viewport, most with a tap-to-call link (24) and a free estimate (22) — and common gaps. Not one shows a price; the only two dollar figures are a discount and financing fine print. 11 show reviews, 5 have an FAQ and 1 prints a licence number. And 26 mention commercial work and 16 cabinets, so a painter who does either well has to show it, not just say it.

Most advice about painting company websites comes from the companies selling them, and it lists everything a website could have. It cannot tell a painter which of those things their competitors already have and which would set them apart. That can be counted — and for painters, two questions come first: does the listing lead to a website at all, and which credential can a painter actually print?

The measurement: 60 listings, 48 painters, six towns

On 23 September 2026 I asked the Brave Search API for its local results for “painters <town> MI” in the eight towns I track — Grand Rapids, Wyoming, Kentwood, Grandville, Walker, Holland, Muskegon and Kalamazoo — and took the first ten listings in each: Brave Search’s local results for “painters <town> MI”, read 23 September 2026 — not Google’s map pack, and not a ranking. Walker returned no listings, as it did for every trade I swept that morning, and for painters Grand Rapids returned none either, so this page covers six towns (three businesses listed for the suburbs have Grand Rapids in their name). The six gave 60 listings. One website is one business: a painter listed in several towns counts once, and a brand whose offices share one website counts once, which leaves 48.

For each listing that names a website I fetched that page once with curl, the way a search crawler or an AI engine does, and read the HTML for a tel: link, LocalBusiness-family schema, an FAQ (FAQPage schema or an FAQ heading), a phone viewport, a contact form, reviews shown (a widget, a star rating or a count), online booking, the towns named, and the phrases a homeowner looks for: a free estimate, quote, consultation, inspection or assessment; financing or monthly payments; emergency or 24/7; “licensed and insured”; a printed licence number; a warranty or guarantee. For painters only: cabinet painting or refinishing, or the word “cabinets”, and the word “commercial”. I then read every dollar figure and licence-style number by hand. That found three phrasings the patterns missed; they were fixed, and every page read again, before the counts below were taken.

Every site is on HTTPS with a phone viewport tag, so neither sets anyone apart. 24 have a tap-to-call link and 22 offer a free estimate, quote or consultation; 20 have both. Roofers, read the same way that morning, were at 37 and 40 of 46 — level on the phone link, well ahead on the estimate. A contact form sits in the HTML of 11 — a floor, since forms drawn by script are invisible to one fetch — and 2 sites show neither a phone link nor a form.

Cabinets and commercial: the two painter-only signals

These two are measured only for painters, and they say different things. The word “commercial” is on 26 of the 30 sites, and on every site read in Wyoming, Holland and Kalamazoo. The pattern is that one word, so it cannot tell a commercial division from “residential and commercial” in a footer — but it does show that saying you do commercial work sets no one apart here. If it is a real part of your business, give it what one word cannot: its own page, the kinds of property you paint, how you work around occupied buildings, and proof a property manager can check.

Cabinets split the field: 16 of the 30 mention cabinet painting or refinishing, or the word “cabinets”; 14 do not. It is a different sale from a repaint — weighed against new cabinets, and bought on kitchen before-and-afters, the finish, how long the kitchen is out of action and a price against replacement. That is a page, not a line: 30 of the 39 top-ranked Grand Rapids trade sites I read on 15 September link at least one service page from the homepage.

The gaps: price, credentials, and the rest

A price. Two of the 30 sites print a dollar figure; I read both by hand (read 23 September 2026), and neither is a price: one is a first-job discount, 10 per cent off up to $500, and the other is financing fine print, monthly payments per $1,000 borrowed with a $50 minimum. Both are on franchise location pages. Not one site says what a room, an exterior or a kitchen’s cabinets cost, even as a range, and cost is the first thing a homeowner wants to know. A range per room or per house, with what moves it — size, ceiling height, prep and repairs, coats, a big colour change — would make you the only painter here who answers it (the case for prices).

A licence, and which one. A roofer should print a Michigan licence number; a painter’s case is different. The Occupational Code lists the trades a residential maintenance and alteration contractor’s licence covers — carpentry, concrete, swimming pool installation, basement waterproofing, excavation, insulation, masonry, roofing, siding and gutters, screen or storm sash, tile and marble, house wrecking — and painting is not among them (MCL 339.2404(3) on legislature.mi.gov, and LARA’s own list, both read 23 September 2026). A painter may still hold a licence, such as a residential builder’s, which anyone can check in LARA’s “Find / Verify a Licensed Professional or Business” lookup (michigan.gov/lara/online-services, read 23 September 2026). 8 of the 30 sites say “licensed and insured”; 1 prints a number. If you hold a licence, print the number and name it; if your point is insurance, name the cover and offer the certificate.

The credential that is about paint. The one an owner of an older house may ask about is federal. EPA’s Renovation, Repair and Painting rule, as EPA sets it out on epa.gov (read 23 September 2026), requires anyone paid for work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes, childcare facilities and preschools built before 1978 to be certified; it names painters among the trades covered and says firms “cannot advertise or perform” that work without firm certification. Michigan is not on EPA’s list of states running their own programme (read 23 September 2026), so here the certification is EPA’s, and EPA has a public search of certified contractors. Minor repair and maintenance (six square feet of paint or less per room inside, 20 outside) and homes built in 1978 or later are exempt. My sweep did not look for it, so I have no count; if you hold it, put it on the homepage with its number. I am not a lawyer, and none of this is advice on what your work requires.

Reviews, an FAQ, booking. 11 of the 30 show reviews on the page — a widget, a printed star rating or a printed count — so 19 give a homeowner no rating without leaving. Someone letting a crew into their home for days wants proof, and a rating with a count, linked to the source, is the quickest (how to show them). 5 have an FAQ, though every estimate raises the same questions: how long, who moves the furniture, which paint, how many coats, the drywall, the weather (why and how). 7 let a visitor book online, worth testing when the estimate is an appointment anyway. 14 of the 30 have none of the three.

Where you work, and weight. The sites name a median of 1 of the 8 sweep towns, and 5 name none; roofers’ sites named a median of 2. Name the towns you actually paint in on the homepage and in your schema, and add a town page only with something true to say about it, not the same page with the name swapped. The median homepage HTML is 181 KB before images and scripts, the largest 1.8 MB — felt on a phone in a driveway (what speed is worth).

What this reading does not prove

  • Brave’s listings, not Google’s map pack. One search engine, six towns, one day: not a ranking, and not every painter in the region.
  • A listing is not the business. “No website listed” means the listing names none, not that the painter has no site; a dead domain may belong to a painter who has moved to a new one.
  • One page each, HTML only. A price elsewhere on the site, or a form or widget drawn by script after load, is invisible here — one site is drawn entirely by script and could not be read at all — and a pattern can miss a phrasing nobody anticipated. Every count is a floor, not a ceiling.
  • Words, not substance. “Commercial” is a word on a page, not a commercial division. The one licence number is a number, not a phrase, but I did not look it up.
  • Correlation, not cause. Nothing here says which element wins jobs; it says what a homeowner comparing these painters will and will not find.
  • The law is summarised, from one section of the Occupational Code, LARA’s trade list and EPA’s pages, all in the receipts, and I am not a lawyer.

Common questions

What should a painting company website have?

First, a listing that leads to it: only 31 of 48 West Michigan painter listings checked on 23 September 2026 led to a live website. Then the baseline — HTTPS, a phone layout, a tap-to-call link and a free estimate (30, 30, 24 and 22 of the 30 sites I could read) — and the gaps most leave open: a price range (0 of 30), reviews on the page (11), an FAQ (5), a page for cabinet or commercial work if you do it, and a credential a homeowner can check.

Should a painter put prices on their website?

A range, yes. None of the 30 West Michigan painter sites read on 23 September 2026 shows a price; the only two dollar figures are a first-job discount and financing fine print. A range per room or per house, with what moves it — size, ceiling height, prep and repairs, coats, a big colour change — answers the first question every homeowner has, and no painter in the sweep answers it.

Does a painter in Michigan need a licence, and what should the website show?

Painting is not among the trades a Michigan maintenance and alteration contractor’s licence covers (MCL 339.2404(3), read 23 September 2026), though a painter may hold a builder’s or another trade’s licence; if you do, print the number and name it. For paid work that disturbs paint in homes built before 1978, EPA’s Renovation, Repair and Painting rule requires certification (epa.gov, read 23 September 2026); if you hold it, show it. Of 30 West Michigan painter sites, 8 say “licensed and insured” and 1 prints a number. I am not a lawyer.
