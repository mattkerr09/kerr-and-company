Is being on page one of Google enough? No — on our own eight sites, positions 4 to 10 earned 9 clicks from 3,228 impressions

“We’ll get you on page one” is the sentence every SEO pitch is built on, and page one for this question is forum threads and agency blogs arguing about it with no numbers. We own eight sites with Search Console data, so on 16 September 2026 I pulled every query for all of them and counted where the clicks actually came from.

By Matthew Kerr Published 16 Sep 2026 Updated 16 Sep 2026 9 min read
Quick answer

No. Page one is ten organic links plus whatever Google puts around them, and the click goes to the top of it. Over the 90 days to 14 September 2026, across the eight Search Console properties we own, the queries where we averaged positions 4 to 10 drew 3,228 impressions and 9 clicks — 0.3%. Positions 11 to 20: 306 impressions, 4 clicks. Positions 51 and beyond: 3,680 impressions, none. One query sat at an average position of 7 with 375 impressions and not one click.

Google counts an impression whether or not your link was scrolled into view, and numbers every element on the page — its own example puts a knowledge panel at position 6 (Search Console Help, read 16 September 2026). So “position 8” can mean under a map pack, a question box and six other links. For a local trade, the calls come from the map pack, which Google ranks on relevance, distance and prominence and says cannot be bought.

What to ask instead of “will we be on page one”: which query, which position, and what appears above it — and for a local business, whether the Business Profile is in the top three of the map. That is what our Local SEO is built around, from $750 a month, three-month initial commitment then month to month, with a live dashboard and everything built in your name.

The honest version of this question is not whether page one matters — it does; page two is where our 3,680 unclicked impressions live — but whether being anywhere on it is worth what an agency is asking. That needs a click curve, and every click curve on the internet belongs to somebody else’s data. Ours is small and it is ours, so this page publishes it whole: eight sites, 1,170 queries, 8,452 impressions, 15 clicks, with the method and the file, and then reads what it means for a Grand Rapids business that has been promised page one.

That is not page one and it is not page two; it is page ten, where an impression is a technicality. It is also exactly what a new site in a market of agencies and directories looks like, and the reason the work on it is the Business Profile and the pages, not a promise about a page number.

What “position” and “page one” mean, in Google’s words

Search Console’s own definitions, read on 16 September 2026 (support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7042828):

  • Position “is typically calculated from top to bottom on the primary side of the page, then top to bottom on the secondary side”, and the number shown “is the topmost position occupied by a link to your property or page in search results, averaged across all queries.”
  • An impression “is counted whenever an item appears in the current page of results, whether or not the item is scrolled into view, as long as the user need not click to see more results.”
  • Every element counts. Google’s own illustration: “Position 6 is occupied by a knowledge panel (a compound element). All links in the panel have position 6.”

Put those together and “we got you to position 8” means: on average, across whatever queries were counted, the first link to your site was the eighth thing on the page, which may have been below a map pack, a question box, an image row and four competitors, and which was credited with an impression even when nobody scrolled to it. An average of 8 can also be position 3 on Tuesday and 13 on Thursday. None of that is dishonest; it is what the number is. It is why the number, on its own, does not predict a phone call.

Being on the page is not being seen. Being seen is not being chosen.

For a Grand Rapids business, the page one that matters is the map

When someone searches for a plumber or a roofer near them, Google says “they’ll find local results on Google Maps and Search”, ranked on three things — relevance (“how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for”), distance, and prominence (“how well-known a business is”) — and, in the same document, “There’s no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google” (support.google.com/business/answer/7091, read 16 September 2026). Those local results are the three businesses in the map pack, and they are what a “page one” promise quietly leaves out: a site can hold an organic position on page one and still sit under three competitors’ phone numbers.

The map pack is built from things you can check yourself. On 15 September 2026 the twelve top-three spots for four Grand Rapids trades held between 22 and 12,142 reviews, and three of the twelve had under 60 (the reviews reading). 40 of 40 top-ranked trades had a website, 30 of 39 linked a page per service, and 32 of 39 had a tap-to-call link. That is the checklist that decides whether the local results include you, and not one item on it is a page number.

What this reading does not prove

  • Fifteen clicks is a small number. These are new sites in narrow niches; the rates are indicative, and a busier site’s curve will be steeper at the top. What the data does show, cleanly, is that thousands of page-one impressions can produce almost nothing.
  • Average position is an average. A query at 8.0 may never have been at 8. The buckets are read as bands, not ranks.
  • Brand queries inflate page-one clicks. Most of the nine clicks at positions 4 to 10 were people searching for a product by name. Strip those and the non-brand rate on page one is lower still.
  • One engine, one window. Google only, 90 days, read once on 16 September 2026; Bing and AI answers are not in it.
  • Nothing here measures the map pack’s own click rate. Search Console reports links to your site; calls and direction requests from the profile are reported inside the profile, not here.
