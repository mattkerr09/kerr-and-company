You can rank without a website. In Grand Rapids, 40 of 40 top-ranked trades have one anyway.

A Google Business Profile with no website can appear in the map pack and ring your phone. Google does not require a site. But on 15 September 2026 I checked the first ten Google Maps listings for plumbers, roofers, HVAC companies and electricians here, and every one of the 40 carried a website link. Here is what the profile does, what it cannot do, and how to decide whether you need both.

What Google says decides local ranking

Google publishes a short help page on how it ranks local results, and I am quoting it rather than paraphrasing it, because paraphrase is how "you need a website to rank" got started. Read on 15 September 2026 at support.google.com/business/answer/7091, it opens with: "Local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity." The three sub-headings that follow are Relevance, Distance and Prominence; the switch from popularity to prominence is Google's wording, not mine.

"Relevance is how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for." "Distance refers to how far each business is from the customer who's searching." And the one that matters for this question: "Prominence means how well-known a business is. Prominent places are more likely to show up in search results. This factor's also based on info like how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have. More reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking."

Read that carefully. Nothing on the page says a website is required. A profile with no site can be relevant, can be close, and can have reviews. What it cannot have is other websites linking to it, because a link needs a page to point at, and a profile is not a page you own. The website is not a ranking factor in itself. It is the thing one of the two prominence signals Google names needs in order to exist.

Google's page on editing a profile (support.google.com/business/answer/3039617, read 15 September 2026) treats the site as something you may add, not something you must have: "You can share your business's website with your customers on your Business Profile." It asks for the full URL including the protocol, and adds: "Make sure that Googlebot isn't blocked on your site." That is the whole of Google's position. Optional, and if you have one, let Google read it.

Read down the verdict column and the shape of the answer is plain. The profile wins on the two things people care about first: showing up and getting the call. The website wins on everything after the click. Which of those matters to you depends on one question, and it is the next section.

When the profile alone is enough

Here is the case nobody on page one states plainly, so I will. If you are a one-person service and your phone already rings as often as you can answer it, you do not need a website. A well-kept profile with steady reviews is doing the job. A site would bring you more enquiries you cannot take, and a monthly bill for the privilege.

That describes a real share of the trades in this city: the electrician who is booked three weeks out, the plumber who only takes referrals, the roofer who does not want to hire. If that is you, keep the profile complete, answer the reviews, and spend nothing. Revisit it when either of two things changes: you can take more work than the profile brings, or customers start asking for a specific service the profile's description box cannot explain.

Forty of forty is not a rule you have to obey. It is what the businesses who wanted more work decided was worth having.

How to decide

Three questions. Answer them honestly and the decision makes itself.

  1. Could you take a fifth more calls next month without hiring? If not, the profile alone is enough for now. Stop here and keep it tidy.
  2. Do customers search for a specific service you offer, not just your trade? "Sump pump replacement", "furnace tune-up", "panel upgrade". Those searches are answered by a page, and a profile does not have pages.
  3. When a searcher compares you to the two listings beside you, what do they tap? On the day I looked, both neighbours had a Website button. If yours does not, the comparison happens without you in it.

Two yeses out of three means build the small site. One or none means the profile is your whole presence, and that is a legitimate choice as long as you make it on purpose.

Common questions

Can a Google Business Profile rank without a website?

Yes. Google's own help page says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and popularity, with the third factor headed prominence, and none of the three requires a website. A complete profile with reviews can appear in the map pack and take calls. What I measured, though, is that on 15 September 2026 every one of the 40 top-ten Google Maps listings across four Grand Rapids trades carried a website link. Ranking without one is possible; in these trades, nobody in the ranked set was doing it that day.

Does adding a website improve local ranking?

Not by itself, and not as a switch. Google's help page says prominence is also based on info like how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have. A website is the thing other sites link to, so it is the path that signal travels along; a profile is not a page anyone links to. Publishing a site and doing nothing else changes little. Publishing service pages that local sites, suppliers and directories link to is what the help page is describing.

What is the cheapest website that is still worth having?

A one-to-three-page static site with a lead form, click-to-call, on-page SEO and the Google setup done: enough to be the page the profile's Website button opens, to explain what you do in your own words, and to catch the people who will not phone. Mine start at $999 one-off, or $149 a month for twelve months after which the site is yours. Because the site is static, it can be handed over and hosted for free.

When is a Google Business Profile enough on its own?

When you are a one-person service with no capacity for more calls. If the phone already rings as often as you can answer it and you are not planning to hire, a well-kept profile with steady reviews is doing the job and a website would mostly add work. Revisit the question when you can take more work than the profile brings, or when customers start asking for a specific service you cannot explain in the profile's description box.
