What should an electrician’s website have? I read 38 West Michigan electricians’ sites, and not one prints its electrical licence number

On 23 September 2026 the first page of results for “what should an electrician website have” was ten lists of best electrician websites and design tips, mostly from software vendors, marketing agencies and theme sellers. None counted what the electricians in one real market put on their sites, so I did that for West Michigan: I took the first ten local listings for electricians in eight towns and read the site each one links to. The first finding came before any site loaded — 19 of the 60 electricians’ listings name no website at all.

By Matthew Kerr Published 23 Sep 2026 Updated 23 Sep 2026 15 min read
Quick answer

The baseline is thin. On 23 September 2026 I read the sites of the electricians Brave Search lists for Grand Rapids, Wyoming, Kentwood, Grandville, Holland, Muskegon and Kalamazoo — 60 businesses, 41 whose listing names a website, 38 of those that could be read. All 38 are on HTTPS and built for phones, and 30 have a tap-to-call link. Nothing else reaches half: the next most common thing is a mention of emergency or 24/7 service, on 18. A site with those three is level with the field, not ahead of it.

The gaps are wide, and the widest costs nothing. Michigan requires an electrical contractor’s licence to engage in the business of electrical contracting (MCL 339.5737, read 23 September 2026); 18 of the 38 sites say they are licensed in some form, and not one prints its electrical licence number. Only 2 show a review rating or count, none shows a price, 3 have an FAQ and 10 carry LocalBusiness schema. Generators are mentioned on 14 sites and EV charging on 9, and 22 of the 38 mention neither.

If you would rather have it done: a Starter Site is from $999 for up to three pages, and a Business Site is $2,499 starting for five to eight pages — room for a page each on emergency work, generators and EV chargers. Every number below is free to use either way.

An electrician’s website does a narrow job. Someone’s power is out, a breaker keeps tripping, or they have just bought an electric car, and what they want to know first is simple: do you do this, are you licensed, and how do I call you. The lists on page one show good-looking sites from across the country, but they cannot tell an electrician in Kentwood what the other electricians a Kentwood homeowner will compare them with actually show. That can be counted. This page counts it for West Michigan, then says what I would put on an electrician’s site as a result.

The measurement: 60 electricians, 7 towns, one page each

The source is Brave Search’s local results for “electricians <town> MI”, read 23 September 2026 between 12:50 and 12:52 UTC — not Google’s map pack. I took the first ten listings in each of the eight towns I track: Grand Rapids, Wyoming, Kentwood, Grandville, Walker, Holland, Muskegon and Kalamazoo. They are the electricians one search engine shows for each town, not a ranking. Walker returned no local listings, as it did for every trade I swept that day, so it is not in the numbers. The other seven towns gave 70 listings; an electrician listed in more than one town counts once, which leaves 60 businesses (9 of them appear in two towns). 41 of the 60 listings name a website and 19 name none. Of the 41 sites, 38 could be read. Two answered with a firewall challenge (HTTP 403) that turns away automated fetches, which usually means a firewall rather than a dead site. The third is drawn entirely by script: the HTML a crawler or an AI engine receives carries a title and not one word, so to anything that is not a person that business has no services, no phone number and no town.

I fetched the page each listing links to once with curl, as a search crawler or an AI engine does — the homepage for 36 of the 38, a town page and a plumbing-services page for the other two — and counted each signal exactly as the code tests it: a tel: link; LocalBusiness-family schema; an FAQ (FAQPage schema or an FAQ heading); a phone viewport; a contact form in the page; a review widget, a printed rating such as “4.8 stars” or a printed count such as “85 Google reviews”; a scheduling widget or a book-online or schedule link; and phrases — “free” before estimate, quote, inspection or consultation; financing or monthly payments; “emergency”, “24/7” or “24-hour”; “licensed and insured”, with or without “bonded”, in its comma and ampersand forms; a number printed after “license” with #, no., number, ID, info or a colon; a warranty or guarantee. Two are for electricians only: the word generator or the brands Generac or Kohler, and EV charging, electric vehicle or car charging. I re-read the emergency, generator, EV, licence and review matches by hand, and every dollar figure. The raw files are in the receipts.

Every town but Walker gave ten listings. Holland’s ten were nine businesses, one listed twice; Wyoming and Kalamazoo each had one site that refused the fetch, and Grandville one drawn entirely by script. The town rows count an electrician once per town, so a business listed in Wyoming and Grandville appears in both rows; the first table counts it once. Two towns stand apart: in Muskegon half the listings name no website, and in Grandville none of the eight sites read mentions emergency or 24-hour service, where five of the six in Grand Rapids do.

One listing in three names no website

The listings said something before any site did. 19 of the 60 name no website: 5 of the 10 in Muskegon, 4 in Grand Rapids, 4 in Kalamazoo, 3 of Holland’s 9 businesses, and one each in Wyoming, Kentwood and Grandville. On the roofers’ listings, read the same morning the same way, it was 2 of 54. That is what the listing names, not proof the business has no site: some of the 19 may have one the listing does not link to, and I did not go looking. But a searcher sees the listing, and a listing with no website link leaves them two choices, the phone or the next listing.

The top of Google’s map looked different. On 15 September I checked the first ten Google Maps listings for “electrician grand rapids mi”, and all ten carried a website link, as did the thirty for plumbers, roofers and HVAC companies (the Business Profile article). Different engine, different day, different list, so the contrast proves nothing on its own; it does say that none of the ten electricians Google put first in Grand Rapids was missing one. If your listing names no website and you have one, fix the listing first — it is the cheapest change on this page.

The licence number: required to trade, printed by none

Michigan’s Skilled Trades Regulation Act says a person shall not engage in the business of electrical contracting unless they hold an electrical contractor’s licence (MCL 339.5737), and the state issues that licence only to a master electrician, or to a business with at least one full-time master electrician who lives in Michigan and is responsible for the code compliance of its installations (MCL 339.5711) — both read on legislature.mi.gov, 23 September 2026. “Licensed and insured” is a phrase any site can print. The licence is a number the state publishes: LARA’s public licensee search, linked from its online-services page under “Find / Verify a Licensed Professional or Business”, searches by licence number or business name and lists Electrical Contractor, Electrical Master and Electrical Journey among its licence types (michigan.gov/lara/online-services, read 23 September 2026).

Reading every mention of a licence by hand, 18 of the 38 sites say they are licensed in some form; 11 of them use the stock phrase “licensed and insured”, with or without “bonded”, which is the tool’s count. Not one prints its electrical licence number. The tool, which looks for a number printed after “license” with #, no., number, ID, info or a colon, found none, and neither did I when I also checked every long number on the 38 pages. The only homepage that prints licence numbers at all belongs to a firm that also does plumbing and heating; the numbers in its footer, for its plumbing, boiler and mechanical trades, fit on one line.

Michigan requires a licence to run an electrical contracting business. Not one of the 38 sites prints its electrical licence number.

So the cheapest improvement on an electrician’s site is also the most checkable: print the number — “Michigan electrical contractor licence no.” and the number — in the footer of every page, and again on each service page. If the master electrician behind the licence is the owner, say so by name; the law ties the contractor’s licence to one. I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice: it is what two sections of the Act say and what 38 sites do.

Emergency, generators and EV chargers: what is particular to electricians

Emergency. Where roofers lead with a free estimate (40 of the 46 roofer sites I read the same morning offered one), electricians lean on the emergency line: 18 of the 38 mention emergency, 24/7 or 24-hour service, and only 8 offer a free estimate, quote or consultation. The words are not always an offer, so I read all 18. Twelve state an emergency or round-the-clock service of their own; in the other six the words come from customer reviews (three), the name of a generator service, a security-monitoring service and a 24/7 answering line. Two of the twelve have no tap-to-call link, so the visitor most likely to be on a phone and in a hurry has to copy the number by hand. If you take calls at night, say the hours in words at the top of the page, next to a tap-to-call link.

Generators. 14 sites mention a generator — the word itself, or the Generac or Kohler brands. Two mention one only inside a customer review, so 12 present generator work as a service, from a line in a list to a section of its own. It is the commoner of the two electrician-specific signals, and the town rows show it most often in Kalamazoo (4 of 5 sites) and in Holland and Grand Rapids (3 of 6 each). It also falls under the licence: the Act’s definition of electrical wiring includes “generating equipment” in or on a building or residence (MCL 339.5701, read 23 September 2026), so a standby-generator page is exactly where a licence number earns its place.

EV chargers. 9 sites mention EV charging or electric vehicles, all nine as a service or a page of its own; 7 of them also mention generators. 16 of the 38 mention at least one of the two jobs and 22 mention neither. If you install either, a page for each — what the job involves, roughly how long it takes, whether it needs a permit (in a municipality that provides inspection, the Act requires one: MCL 339.5731, read 23 September 2026) and your licence number — answers what a homeowner searching for that job wants to know.

Reviews, prices and the rest of the gaps

Reviews. As the tool counts them — a review widget, a printed rating or a printed review count — reviews appear on 2 of the 38 sites. Reading the pages by hand, at least 11 more quote a customer in the page text, usually a sentence and a first name, with no rating or count beside it; I found no customer’s words at all on the other 25. A quoted sentence is better than nothing, but a visitor cannot check it. A star rating and a count linked to your Google profile they can, and the honest ways to show them are on the reviews page.

A price. No site shows one. Three print a dollar figure, and I read each by hand (read 23 September 2026): a coupon for $35 off any repair and one for $20 off drain cleaning, both from firms that also do plumbing, and a customer review saying the work cost $700 less than another electrician’s quote. Two offers and a compliment; none tells a homeowner what an outlet, a panel upgrade or a charger installation costs. A starting price for your three most common jobs, with what moves it, would make you the only electrician in this set that shows one (the case for prices).

Schema, forms, FAQs and booking. 10 sites carry LocalBusiness-type schema, the markup that tells a search engine what the business is and where (what it does). 12 have a contact form in the HTML and 30 a tap-to-call link; 5 have neither that a crawler can see. 7 offer a book-online or schedule link. 3 have an FAQ, the cheapest place to answer what every caller asks: whether the job needs a permit, how long it takes, and whether you come out for small jobs (why and how). 3 mention financing and 4 a warranty or guarantee.

Where you work, and page weight. The sites name a median of 1 of the 8 towns they were listed in; 7 name none and 3 name four or more. A homeowner in Holland wants to see Holland, so name the towns you serve on the homepage and in your schema. The median page is 155 KB of HTML before images and scripts, and the largest almost 1 MB.

What this reading does not prove

  • Brave’s listings, not Google’s map pack. These are the electricians one search engine lists for each town on one day. They are not a ranking and not every electrician in the region.
  • Not all of them wire houses. The listings include industrial contractors, an electric-motor repair shop, an electrical supply house and two firms that also do plumbing and heating, one of whose listings links to a plumbing page. I kept every one, because a homeowner comparing sites sees them too — which is itself a reason to say plainly on your homepage that you work on homes, if you do.
  • One page, HTML only. The page each listing links to, fetched once. A licence number on an About page, a price on a service page, or a review widget drawn by script after the page loads is invisible here, so every count is a floor, not a ceiling.
  • Words, not substance. “Mentions generators” means the word is on the page, not that the work is good. I read the emergency, generator, EV, licence, review and dollar matches by hand, not every signal, and I did not look any licence up.
  • Correlation, not cause. Nothing here says which element wins jobs. It says what a homeowner comparing these sites will and will not find.
  • The law is summarised, from the sections of the Skilled Trades Regulation Act linked in the receipts, and I am not a lawyer.

Common questions

What should an electrician’s website have?

The baseline West Michigan electricians already have — HTTPS, a phone-ready layout and a tap-to-call link (38, 38 and 30 of the 38 sites read on 23 September 2026) — then the gaps most of them leave open: your Michigan electrical licence number (not one of the 38 prints it), a review rating and count (2), an FAQ (3), LocalBusiness schema (10), a starting price for common jobs (none), the towns you serve by name, and a page for each service you want found, such as generators (mentioned on 14) and EV charging (9).

Should an electrician put their licence number on their website?

Yes. Michigan’s Skilled Trades Regulation Act requires an electrical contractor’s licence to engage in the business of electrical contracting (MCL 339.5737, read 23 September 2026), and LARA’s public licensee search finds a licence by its number. Of the 38 West Michigan electrician sites read that day, 18 say they are licensed in some form and not one prints its electrical licence number. Put it in the footer of every page and on each service page. I am not a lawyer; that is what the Act says and what the sites do.

Should an electrician’s website have pages for generators and EV chargers?

If you do the work, yes. Of 38 West Michigan electrician sites read on 23 September 2026, 14 mention generators, 9 mention EV charging and 22 mention neither. A page for each, saying what the job involves, roughly how long it takes and whether it needs a permit, answers what a homeowner searching for that job wants to know. The Act’s definition of electrical wiring includes generating equipment (MCL 339.5701, read 23 September 2026), so put your licence number on those pages too.
