What should a landscaping website have? I checked 62 West Michigan landscapers: 13 listings name no website, and half the sites I could read mention snow removal

On 23 September 2026 the first page of results for “what should a landscaping website have” was ten pages from website builders, theme shops, software makers and marketing agencies: feature lists and good-looking examples. Not one of the nine I could open counts anything about a single local market. So I did: I took the first ten local listings for landscapers in eight West Michigan towns, followed every website link, and read every homepage I could.

By Matthew Kerr Published 23 Sep 2026 Updated 23 Sep 2026 16 min read
Quick answer

Start before the homepage. On 23 September 2026 I checked the landscapers Brave Search lists for Grand Rapids, Wyoming, Kentwood, Grandville, Holland, Muskegon and Kalamazoo — 70 listings, 62 businesses. 13 of those listings name no website, 2 link a Facebook page instead of a website, one website address now forwards to a gambling-spam page, and 3 more sites could not be read. A landscaper’s first job is a website the listing actually names, and that loads.

On the 43 homepages I could read, the baseline is HTTPS (all 43), a phone layout (42) and a tap-to-call link (30). The trade’s two seasons show plainly: 22 mention snow removal or plowing and 20 mention landscape design or hardscaping. What helps a homeowner choose is rare: 14 offer a free estimate or quote, 4 show reviews on the page, 2 have an FAQ, 1 shows a price for landscaping work and none prints a licence number.

So a landscaping website should have the baseline, a page for each season it sells, and as many of those gaps closed as are true for the business. That is how we build them: our contractor websites start at $999 for up to three pages, and the Business Site ($2,499 starting, 5–8 pages) has room for separate lawn, design and snow pages, with local schema and reviews. The count below is free to use whether or not you ever talk to us.

Most advice about landscaping websites comes from people selling something to landscapers — themes, site builders, scheduling software, marketing — and it is written for a landscaper anywhere. What would set a Kentwood landscaper apart depends on what the other landscapers a Kentwood homeowner compares already have, and that can be counted. This page counts it for West Michigan, then says what I would put on a landscaping site as a result.

The measurement: 70 listings, 62 landscapers, 43 homepages

On 23 September 2026 I asked the Brave Search API for its local results for “landscapers <town> MI” in the eight towns I track — Grand Rapids, Wyoming, Kentwood, Grandville, Walker, Holland, Muskegon and Kalamazoo — and took the first ten listings in each, the same method as my read of roofer sites that day. These are Brave’s local listings, not Google’s map pack: the landscapers a search engine shows for the town, not a ranking. Walker returned no local listings at all, so it is not in the numbers. The other seven gave 70 listings. Kentwood’s ten included one business listed twice, and four businesses appear in more than one town — one in four towns, one in three and two in two — so each is counted once, which leaves 62 landscapers.

13 of the 62 listings name no website, and 2 link a Facebook page instead of a website, which leaves 47 with a website address of their own. Four of those did not give me a homepage I could read (more on them below); the other 43 are what the tables count.

The first gap is before the homepage

13 of the 62 listings name no website at all. That does not mean those 13 businesses have none — a listing can simply leave the field empty — but a homeowner who finds them through this search has nothing to tap except the phone number. The top of Google’s map was different for the four trades I read there on 15 September: all 40 of the top-ten listings for Grand Rapids plumbers, roofers, HVAC companies and electricians had a website link. Here, a website that the listing names and that loads on a phone already puts a landscaper ahead of a fifth of the field.

Two more listings link a Facebook page instead of a website. A Facebook page can show photos, but it is Facebook’s page, laid out by Facebook: it answered my fetch of both with an error (HTTP 400), so neither could be read the way the other 43 were.

And four website addresses did not give me a homepage I could read, each for a reason worth knowing. One listing’s address now forwards to an unrelated gambling-spam page in another language. The domain registry’s public record, read 23 September 2026, shows the current registration of that domain began on 13 July 2026 — the pattern of a name that lapsed and was bought by someone else. A homeowner who taps that listing’s website link lands on a gambling page. Another site’s security certificate does not match its own domain, so browsers show a warning page first, and a third server did not answer at all. The fourth is drawn entirely by script: the HTML a single fetch receives is an empty frame with a title and schema markup and no page text, so a crawler or AI engine that reads the HTML without running the scripts gets nothing to read. Check what your listing’s website link opens, on a phone, and make sure the domain is registered to you and set to renew.

Two businesses on one homepage: snow, and design and hardscape

Each landscaping signal covers about half the field. 22 of the 43 homepages mention snow removal, snow plowing or a snow plow; counting the word snow in any form (“snow & ice management”, for example), 26 do. 20 mention landscape design or hardscaping; counting patios, pavers or retaining walls as well, 25 do. Those wider counts are mine, by hand; the table uses the tool’s exact phrases. 12 homepages mention both snow and design, 10 only snow, 8 only design or hardscape, and 13 neither. Half the landscapers here are selling two different things from one homepage: green work in the growing season, and snow in winter.

The snow half is where speed matters, and the homepages mostly allow for it: 16 of the 22 have a tap-to-call link. But only 4 of the 22 offer a book-online or schedule link, and 3 mention 24/7 or 24-hour service. Someone looking for plowing wants three answers before the first storm: whether you take residential work, commercial work or both, which areas you cover, and how to get on the list. A separate snow page that answers those, with its own call button and form, does more than a line in the services menu.

The design and hardscape half is where the homepages say least. Of the 20 that mention landscape design or hardscaping, none has an FAQ, 1 shows reviews on the page, 1 mentions financing and 7 offer a free estimate or quote. A patio, a wall or a planting plan is a bigger decision than a weekly mow, and it is where a homeowner most wants to see cost, proof and a promise before calling. 12 of the 20 do point to a gallery, a portfolio or “our work” (my count, by hand). What is missing is a starting price for a typical job, reviews beside the photos, and a warranty stated plainly: 11 of the 43 homepages use the word warranty or guarantee, but only 3 point to a warranty on the work or the plants (one names a one-year plant warranty); 5 offer a satisfaction guarantee and 3 use the word in passing.

Of the 20 homepages that mention design or hardscaping, none has an FAQ and 1 shows reviews.

The gaps: what almost no landscaper does

A free estimate. 14 of 43 homepages offer a free estimate, quote or consultation, and nine of them call it a free quote. If you quote for free, say so on the homepage: it costs nothing, and 29 of 43 do not.

A price. Three homepages print a dollar figure, and I read each one by hand (read 23 September 2026). One is a price for landscaping work: a $99 minimum charge for stump grinding. One is a $40 cash reward for referring a new customer, which is an offer, not a price. The third is a catalogue of street signs, address plaques and post lights from $54 to $304: that business makes signs, and Brave lists it among landscapers, so its prices are real but not for landscaping. That leaves 42 of 43 homepages giving no idea what mowing, a spring cleanup or a patio costs. A starting price per visit for recurring work and a typical range for design and hardscape jobs, with what moves it, is enough; the case for it is on the prices page.

Reviews and an FAQ. 4 of 43 show reviews on the page — three landscapers with a review widget, and the sign maker’s shop reviews — so 39 give a visitor comparing landscapers no rating to see without leaving. The honest ways to show them are on the reviews page. 2 have an FAQ, which is the cheapest place to answer what a homeowner asks before calling: what a season costs, whether there is a contract, what gets sprayed, and when snow service starts (why and how).

A next step besides the phone. 11 homepages carry a contact form in their HTML and 5 a book-online or schedule link; 11 show a crawler none of the three — no tap-to-call link, form or booking link — though ten of those eleven link to a separate contact page, one tap further away. Only one homepage has a text-message link, and it is a national chain’s line for existing accounts. In a trade where the quickest way to describe the job is a photo of the yard, a text link is an easy win.

Where you work. The homepages name a median of 1 of the eight towns in this sweep, and 7 name none of them. Several of these landscapers are based outside the eight, so a low count is not always a fault, but a homeowner in Wyoming wants to see Wyoming. Name the towns you serve on the homepage and in your schema — only 16 of the 43 carry LocalBusiness-type schema at all (what schema does) — and build a page per town only when you have something true to say about each one, not the same page with the name swapped.

Licences: what Michigan law says, and what 43 homepages print

3 of the 43 homepages say “licensed and insured”, and none prints a licence number; I also searched every homepage for a number near the word licence and found none. Whether a landscaper needs a state licence depends on the work, and two Michigan laws matter. I am not a lawyer, so what follows is what the statutes say, not advice about your business.

The Occupational Code’s definition of a residential maintenance and alteration contractor covers paid repair or improvement of a house and also “laying of concrete on residential property” (MCL 339.2401); the crafts that licence covers include concrete, excavation and masonry work (MCL 339.2404); and its small-job exemption applies only to a project whose total price is under $600 (MCL 339.2403), all three read 23 September 2026 on legislature.mi.gov. Mowing and lawn care are not among the listed crafts. Whether a given patio, wall or grading job needs a licence is a question for LARA, the state licensing agency, which offers a public “Verify a License/Registration for a Health or Occupational Code Professional” lookup (michigan.gov/lara/online-services, read 23 September 2026).

Lawn treatment falls under a different law. Michigan’s Natural Resources and Environmental Protection Act counts weeds and insects as pests (MCL 324.8305), requires a commercial applicator that holds itself out as being in the business of applying pesticides to hold a commercial applicator licence for each place of business (MCL 324.8313), and, with narrow exceptions, lets only certified or registered applicators apply a pesticide for a commercial purpose (MCL 324.8314), all read 23 September 2026 on legislature.mi.gov. The Michigan Department of Agriculture and Rural Development, which runs that licensing, says applicators must also obtain a pesticide application business licence to apply pesticides for hire, and links a report of the businesses currently licensed (michigan.gov/mdard, read 23 September 2026).

So I read the 43 homepages for this too, by hand. 10 use the words “weed control” and 18 mention fertilizing in some form; two link to a page of pesticide information. None uses the word “applicator”, none names the state agriculture department, and none prints a licence number of any kind. If you spray, your licence is the most checkable trust signal you have, and nobody here shows it: put the business licence on the site, say who on the crew is certified, and say what you apply.

What this reading does not prove

  • Brave’s listings, not Google’s map pack. These are the landscapers one search engine lists for each town on one day — not a ranking, not every landscaper in the region, and not a clean list: it included a sign maker and the local pages of two national lawn-care brands.
  • A listing without a website is not a business without one. The 13 are listings that name no website; some of those businesses may have a site the listing does not show.
  • Homepages only, HTML only. A price on another page, or a form, a review widget or a whole page drawn by script after load, is invisible here; one homepage could not be read at all for that reason. Every count is a floor, not a ceiling.
  • Words, not substance. “Mentions snow removal” means the words are on the page, not that the service is good. Where I counted wider wording by hand, I say so.
  • Correlation, not cause. Nothing here says which element wins jobs. It says what a homeowner comparing these sites will and will not find.
  • The law is summarised, from the sections of the two Michigan laws linked in the receipts, and I am not a lawyer.

Common questions

What should a landscaping company website have?

First, a website your listing actually names: 13 of 62 West Michigan landscaper listings checked on 23 September 2026 name none. Then the baseline the others share — HTTPS, a phone layout and a tap-to-call link (43, 42 and 30 of the 43 homepages read) — and a page for each season you sell: 22 mention snow removal and 20 landscape design or hardscaping. Then the gaps almost nobody closes: a free estimate or quote (14 of 43), reviews on the page (4), an FAQ (2), a price (1) and a licence number (0).

Should a landscaper put prices on their website?

A starting price, yes. Of 43 West Michigan landscaping homepages read 23 September 2026, only 1 shows a price for landscaping work: a $99 minimum charge for stump grinding. The other 42 leave a homeowner guessing about mowing, cleanups and patios alike. Give a starting price per visit for recurring work and a typical range for design and hardscape jobs, with what moves it: lot size, access and materials.

Does a landscaper need a licence in Michigan?

It depends on the work, and I am not a lawyer. Michigan’s Occupational Code names “laying of concrete on residential property” in its definition of a residential maintenance and alteration contractor (MCL 339.2401), and a business that holds itself out as applying pesticides — weeds count as pests — needs a commercial applicator licence for each place of business (MCL 324.8305 and 324.8313), all read 23 September 2026. None of the 43 West Michigan landscaping homepages read that day prints a licence number.
