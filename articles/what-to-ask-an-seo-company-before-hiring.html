What to ask an SEO company before hiring: 8 Grand Rapids agency pages answer 5 of 40 questions before the call — and none says who owns the work

Page one for this question is ten lists of “questions to ask”, none of which checked whether the agencies a Grand Rapids business would call have already answered any of them. So on 16 September 2026 I read the eight local agency pages a search returns and scored each against five questions a business owner needs answered before signing.

By Matthew Kerr Published 16 Sep 2026 Updated 16 Sep 2026 10 min read
Quick answer

Ask five things, in writing: what it costs, how long you are signed for, how long until results, who owns the work if you leave, and how often you get a report. On 16 September 2026 I read the Grand Rapids SEO page of each of the eight agencies on page one for seo company grand rapids and checked which of the five each page answers before you call. Eight pages, forty possible answers, five given. One agency (helium-seo.com) answers four; one (rankworks.com) answers one; six answer none. No page says who owns the pages, profile and accounts if you go.

Google’s own guidance for hiring an SEO, read the same day, is that an audit should give you “realistic estimates of improvement, and an estimate of the work involved”, and that if they guarantee first place, “find someone else.” Treat “it depends” as a no until it is a number on paper.

Ours are on the SEO page: Local SEO from $750 a month, a three-month initial commitment then month to month, local movement in four to eight weeks and competitive terms in four to six months, a live dashboard instead of a monthly PDF, and you own every page, profile and asset we build, even if you leave.

Every list of “questions to ask an SEO agency” assumes you will get the answers on a call, and most of them are twenty-five questions long, which is a list nobody asks. This page cuts it to the five whose answers decide whether a bad month costs you a month or a year, and then measures the only thing about them that can be measured from outside: whether the agencies a Grand Rapids business will actually find have put the answers on the page where you would look first. The same eight pages were read earlier for contracts and timelines; this reading adds ownership and reporting and scores all five together.

The five questions, and what a good answer looks like

  1. What does it cost, and what is in that number? A monthly figure or a starting figure on the page, and a list of what it buys. “Depends on scope” is true and is not an answer; a starting price with the scope it covers is.
  2. How long am I signed for? The initial term, whether it then goes month to month, and the notice period. Three months is the shortest window any local page quotes for movement; twelve months of lock-in protects revenue, not results.
  3. How long until I should see something? A range, with what you can check yourself at month one and month three — pages indexed, profile complete, citations live, review count moving. A date for page one is the answer Google says to walk away from.
  4. Who owns the work if I leave? Domain, site, content, Google Business Profile, Search Console, analytics, ad accounts. The answer should be “you”, with you as the primary owner of each and the agency as a manager or user. This is the one the reading found nobody answers.
  5. How often do I get a report, and of what? A cadence — weekly, monthly — and the contents: clicks and calls, not impressions and “visibility”. A dashboard you can open yourself beats a PDF you cannot.

Three readings. First, the one agency that answers is the one that answers almost everything: helium-seo.com puts its starting price, its month-to-month terms, its three-to-six-month window and its reporting cadence on the same page, which is what a page built for a buyer looks like. Third, the column that comes up empty is the one with the most money in it. Five of forty answers are on the page. The other thirty-five are on the call, after you have given your number.

What the pages say instead

Where a page does not answer, it usually says something else in the same place, and the substitutions are worth knowing so you recognise them on the call.

  • A budget instead of a price. Thrive’s Grand Rapids page carries no price, but its proposal forms ask for an “Estimated Monthly Budget” starting at “$2,500-$5,000 per month” on one form and a “Monthly Marketing Budget” starting at “Under $750” on another (thriveagency.com, read 16 September 2026). The question is being asked in the other direction.
  • A promise instead of a timeline. Elit-web’s page states no term, price or timeline but says “We will increase the number of applications by at least 15-20%”, “Reduce the click price by 25-30%” and “Decrease the conversion price by at least 20%” (elit-web.com, read 16 September 2026). Those are outcomes no agency controls, and a percentage with no baseline, no query and no date is not something you can hold anyone to.
  • An average instead of your case. RankWorks says its Grand Rapids clients “see an average of 245% increase in organic traffic and 290% improvement in local search rankings within 6 months” (rankworks.com, read 16 September 2026). The page does not say how many clients, from what base, or what a 290% improvement in a ranking is. It is quoted here as the page’s claim about its own work, nothing more.
  • Values instead of terms. Thrive lists “Honesty”, “Transparent Reporting” and “Timely Delivery”; JSL warns of agencies that “collect a check every month and do little in return” and promises “straightforward reporting”. Both may be true. Neither is a price, a term, a cadence or an ownership clause.
  • A link instead of an answer. JSL links to a “pricing request”; Digital Piloto to “SEO Packages”. I followed the second: the packages page shows a form and no prices (digitalpiloto.com/seo-package-prices/, read 16 September 2026).

Google’s own line on the one question that should end the call

Google publishes guidance for businesses hiring an SEO, and two sentences of it settle the guarantee question. On what you should get: “An SEO audit should be about giving you realistic estimates of improvement, and an estimate of the work involved.” On what should end the conversation: “If they guarantee you that their changes will give you first place in search results, find someone else” (developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo, read 16 September 2026). None of the eight pages guarantees a ranking. One promises percentages, which is the same instinct with a different unit. And “page one” itself is a weaker promise than it sounds: on our own sites, positions 4 to 10 earned nine clicks from 3,228 impressions.

What this reading does not prove

  • Pages are not calls. An agency that answers none of the five on its page may answer all five, honestly, in the first ten minutes. The measurement is what is written before the call, because that is what a business owner can compare across eight agencies without booking eight calls.
  • One page per agency, served HTML only. A term on a services page I did not open, or a price added by a script after load, would be missed. Digital Piloto was read through a fetch service after a direct request was refused.
  • Page one, one engine, one day. These eight are who a Grand Rapids business finds with that search on 16 September 2026; they are not the market, and the set is the same as the two earlier readings by design.
  • The quoted claims are the agencies’ own. Percentages, averages and timelines above are what each page says about its own work; none was checked against a client’s data and this page does not say any of them is wrong.
  • No agency is being accused of anything. Every cell is what the named page said or did not say on that date, with the address in the receipts.

Common questions

What questions should I ask an SEO company before hiring?

Five, in writing: what it costs per month and what is in that number; how long you are signed for and what notice ends it; how long until you should expect movement and what you can check yourself at month one and month three; who owns the pages, the Google Business Profile, the analytics and the content if you leave; and how often you get a report and what is in it. On 16 September 2026 the eight Grand Rapids SEO agency pages on page one for the search answered 5 of those 40 possible answers before the call — one agency four, one agency one, six none. The answers exist; you have to ask for them.

Should an SEO company guarantee rankings?

No, and Google says so in its own guidance for people hiring one, read 16 September 2026: an audit should give you realistic estimates of improvement and of the work involved, and if they guarantee you that their changes will give you first place in search results, find someone else. Of the eight Grand Rapids agency pages read the same day, none guarantees a ranking; one promises percentage increases in enquiries and decreases in click costs, which is an outcome no agency controls. Treat a promised number as a reason to ask how it will be measured, not as a reason to sign.

Who should own the website and Google Business Profile when you hire an SEO company?

You. The domain in your registrar account, the site on hosting you can log into, the Google Business Profile with you as primary owner and the agency added as a manager, Search Console and analytics on your Google account with the agency as a user, and the content written for you yours to keep. None of the eight Grand Rapids agency pages read on 16 September 2026 says any of this either way, so it has to be asked and written into the agreement.
