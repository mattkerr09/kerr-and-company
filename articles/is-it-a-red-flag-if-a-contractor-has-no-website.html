Is it a red flag if a contractor has no website? Not by itself — 76 of 384 West Michigan contractor listings name none

“My handyman doesn’t have a website — is that a red flag?” is a question homeowners keep asking in forums, and on 23 September 2026 the first page of results for it was those forum threads and lists of warning signs, none with a number in them. So I counted: of the contractors a search actually shows in West Michigan, how many list no website, how many list one that does not work, and what a homeowner can check instead.

By Matthew Kerr Published 23 Sep 2026 Updated 23 Sep 2026 9 min read
Quick answer

Not by itself. On 23 September 2026 I read the first ten local listings Brave Search shows for seven trades — roofers, plumbers, heating and cooling companies, electricians, landscapers, painters and remodelers — in seven West Michigan towns: 384 contractors, each counted once. 76 of their listings (20%) name no website at all, from 19 of 60 electricians to 2 of 54 roofers, and 4 more link a Facebook or Instagram page instead. A missing website tells you more about the trade than about the work.

Two things tell you more. First, whether the website a listing names actually works: of the 304 it names, 24 did not show the business when I tried them — a domain that no longer exists, a page that errors or times out, or a page that is not theirs at all (3 parked domains, 2 redirects to someone else's site and 1 placeholder page). Second, the licence: Michigan licenses the trades homeowners hire most — residential builders and maintenance and alteration contractors (MCL 339.2401; projects under $600 are exempt, MCL 339.2403), plumbers (MCL 339.6107), electrical contractors (MCL 339.5737) and the mechanical contractors who do heating and cooling (MCL 339.5809), all read 23 September 2026 on legislature.mi.gov — and the state’s public lookup shows any licence in a minute.

A website is not a licence, a reference or a warranty. Plenty of good tradespeople run on referrals and a phone number, and plenty of bad ones have a slick site. What a website does is let a stranger check you before calling, and what its absence does is move that checking somewhere else — to the licence, the reviews and the written estimate. This page counts how common the missing or broken website really is in West Michigan, then lists the checks that matter more, with the law they rest on.

The measurement: 384 contractors, seven trades, seven towns

On 23 September 2026 I asked the Brave Search API for its local results for “<trade> <town> MI” for seven trades in eight towns — Grand Rapids, Wyoming, Kentwood, Grandville, Walker, Holland, Muskegon and Kalamazoo — and took the first ten listings each time. These are Brave’s local listings, not Google’s map pack. Walker returned no listings for any trade and painters returned none in Grand Rapids, so those are not in the numbers. A contractor listed in several towns, or twice under the same name, counts once. For every listing that names a website I fetched the homepage once with curl and checked whether it actually shows the business. “No website” below means the listing names none — the business may still have one the listing does not show.

“Did not show the business” counts domains that no longer resolve, links to pages that no longer exist, pages that timed out or returned an error, and domains that now show a parked page, a blank page, a placeholder or someone else’s site. It leaves out 9 sites that refused an automated fetch or are drawn by script after they load, because those usually work for a person.

Which trades skip the website

The spread is wide. Electricians are the most likely to have a listing with no website (19 of 60); roofers are the least (2 of 54). I did not measure why, and there are several ordinary explanations — some trades get most of their work through general contractors, commercial accounts or repeat customers, and never needed a public website to fill the calendar. The practical point for a homeowner is that in some trades a listing without a website is normal, so on its own it is weak evidence of anything.

A listing whose website shows someone else’s page is a stronger warning than a listing with no website at all.

When the website is worse than none

The more telling cases are the listings that name a website that does not show the business. 24 of the 304 named websites (8%) failed that way on 23 September: 9 domain names that no longer resolve, 2 links to a page that no longer exists, 3 that timed out on every try, 4 that returned an error or refused the connection and 6 that load but are not the business’s own site (3 parked domains, 2 redirects to someone else's site and 1 placeholder page). A lapsed domain is the common thread: when a business stops paying for its domain, someone else can buy it, and the listing keeps sending customers there. For a homeowner, a listing that points to a dead or taken-over domain says the business is not watching its own details — which is worth a question before you hand over a deposit. For the contractor, it means the search listing is sending people to a stranger.

What to check instead (about five minutes)

  • The licence. Michigan’s Occupational Code covers anyone paid to build, repair or improve a house — a residential builder or a residential maintenance and alteration contractor (MCL 339.2401) — and exempts only a project whose total price is under $600 (MCL 339.2403). Plumbing work requires a licensed plumbing contractor and plumbers (MCL 339.6107), electrical contracting requires an electrical contractor’s licence and only licensed individuals may install wiring (MCL 339.5737, 339.5731), and heating and cooling work is licensed as mechanical work (MCL 339.5809). All five sections were read 23 September 2026 on legislature.mi.gov. The state agency, LARA, runs a public “Find / Verify a Licensed Professional or Business” lookup (michigan.gov/lara/online-services, read 23 September 2026). Ask for the licence number and look it up.
  • The reviews on their Google listing, read for the pattern rather than the star average: dates, detail, how the owner answers a complaint.
  • A written estimate that names the work, the materials and the price before any deposit.
  • Proof of insurance — ask for a certificate from their insurer rather than a line on a website.

None of those four needs a website, and each one says more than a website does. None of this is legal advice; it is what the statutes say, with the date I read them, and I am not a lawyer.

What this reading does not prove

  • Brave’s listings, one day. These are the contractors one search engine lists for each town on 23 September 2026, not every contractor in West Michigan.
  • “No website” means the listing names none. Some of these businesses may have a site the listing does not show.
  • One fetch per site. A server that failed may be back tomorrow; the domains that no longer resolve and the pages that are not the business’s own were checked again by hand the same day.
  • No link to quality. Nothing here measures workmanship. A website, or the lack of one, is not evidence of how the job will go.

Common questions

Is it a red flag if a contractor doesn’t have a website?

Not by itself. Of 384 West Michigan contractors read on 23 September 2026, 76 (20%) have a search listing that names no website, and in some trades that is normal. Check the Michigan licence in LARA’s public lookup, the reviews on their Google listing, a written estimate and an insurance certificate instead.

What is a bigger warning sign than no website?

A listing whose website does not show the business. On 23 September 2026, 24 of the 304 websites named by West Michigan contractor listings did not: dead domains, failed servers, parked or blank pages, placeholders, and lapsed domains that now show someone else’s site. It means nobody is watching the listing, which is worth a question before any deposit.

How do I check a contractor’s licence in Michigan?

Ask for the licence number and look it up in LARA’s “Find / Verify a Licensed Professional or Business” service (michigan.gov/lara/online-services, read 23 September 2026). Michigan licenses residential builders and maintenance and alteration contractors (MCL 339.2401; projects under $600 are exempt, MCL 339.2403), plumbers (MCL 339.6107), electrical contractors (MCL 339.5737) and mechanical contractors (MCL 339.5809).
