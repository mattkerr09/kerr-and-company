Why do plumber website prices vary so much? Because a $39-a-month lease and a $24,000 build are different purchases — 9 seller pages read 16 September 2026

Page one for this question is mostly about why plumbers charge what they do, and the few pages about websites quote a range and move on. Nobody laid the sellers’ own pricing pages side by side, so on 16 September 2026 I opened thirteen of them.

By Matthew Kerr Published 16 Sep 2026 Updated 16 Sep 2026 10 min read
Quick answer

Because the sellers are selling different things under one name. On 16 September 2026 I opened thirteen plumber-website seller pages from page one of three searches; nine state a price. They run from a $39-a-month lease to $24,000 paid over twelve months, and the number of pages you get is the smallest part of that gap.

Three things move the price. Whether you own the site when you stop paying: four of the nine say you do, one says the code cannot leave its platform, four do not say. What is bundled with it: hosting and edits, a Google Business Profile, SEO, ad management. And the term: a 90-day minimum, month to month, twelve monthly payments, or paid once.

Ours is on the page: $999 once for up to three pages, or $149 a month for twelve months with hosting, updates and edits included and the site yours at month twelve; five to eight pages with copywriting from $2,499. You own the code, content and domain either way.

A plumber who searches for a website price finds numbers that do not seem to describe the same product, because they do not. One page wants $39 a month. One wants $300 down and $297 a month with a 90-day minimum. One wants $3,000 and then $150 a month for as long as it hosts the site. One wants $2,000 a month for twelve months and says so plainly. Every one of those is a plumber website. This page puts the sellers’ own words in one table, then says which three questions explain the spread — and they are the same three questions you should ask before paying anyone, including me.

Read down the ownership column and the spread starts to make sense. The cheapest three rows are subscriptions to a platform or a lease, and the one that explains itself says the code stays put. The dearest row says in the same breath that the site is yours outright. The middle of the table is where the surprises live: a $297-a-month plan with a 90-day minimum that does not say who owns the site, and a $150-a-month hosting line that lasts “as long as we host it”. None of that is hidden. It is on the page, in words a plumber can read, and it is why two quotes for “a website” can be a year apart in cost.

The three things that move the price

1. Whether it is yours on the day you stop paying

This is the biggest line, and it is the one most sellers leave for the call. Four of the nine say the site is yours; one says the code cannot leave; four say nothing. A subscription site at $39 to $99 a month is honest value for a first year if you know that the day you cancel, the site comes down and you start again. It is a bad deal if you thought you were paying off a purchase. Who owns your website and domain is how to check what you actually hold, and the domain is the part that must be in your name whatever the rest says — one of the platforms above tells you the same thing on its own FAQ.

2. What is riding along with the site

Six of the nine include hosting in the monthly figure. Beyond that the bundles diverge: edits (30 minutes a month at one seller, unlimited at two), a Google Business Profile setup, weekly blog posts, call tracking, ad management. A $1,200-a-month figure that includes Local SEO and Business Profile work is not a website price at all; it is a marketing retainer with a site attached, and it should be compared with what Grand Rapids agencies charge and with an SEO retainer priced on its own, not with a $500 three-page build. When a figure looks high, find the line that says what else is in it.

3. The term

Seven of the nine state a term. Month to month with notice is the common shape; one asks a 90-day minimum; one splits a fixed total into twelve payments, which is a purchase on instalments, not a subscription; one is paid once. Read “no contract” carefully: it means you can stop, not that anything transfers when you do. The same reading on SEO retainers is on the contracts page, where one of eight Grand Rapids agency pages stated its term.

Two quotes for “a website” can be a year apart in cost and both be honest. The word is doing too much work.

What barely moves it: the pages

Only three of the nine sellers say how many pages you get, and where they do, the counts are the ones you would guess: three, five to eight, eight, ten to fifteen. A page count is the thing you can control and the thing that should set the price of the build itself — it is the main driver on our own price list — but across these nine pages it is not what separates $468 from $24,000. Term and ownership are. What this reading does not prove

  • Silence is not a policy. A page that does not say who owns the site may belong to a seller that hands over everything, or one that does not. The measurement is what is written before the call, because that is what a plumber can compare; four of nine silent means four of nine make you ask.
  • One page per seller. A term or an ownership clause on a page I did not open would be missed. Where a pricing page linked to an FAQ on the same page, I read it; I did not search each site.
  • Page one, one engine, one day. Nine sellers is who a plumber finds with those three searches on 16 September 2026; it is not the market, and two named sellers could not be read that day because their pricing addresses returned 404.
  • The first-year column is mine. It is the seller’s stated rate times twelve plus any stated upfront amount, so you can compare rows; discounts, prepayment offers and tiers above the first are not in it. Roundhouse, for instance, waives its start-up fee for three months prepaid, per its page.
  • No seller is being accused of anything. Every cell is what the named page said or did not say on 16 September 2026, with the address in the receipts so you can read it yourself.

Common questions

Why do plumber website prices vary so much?

Because the sellers are selling different things under one name. Of thirteen plumber-website seller pages read on 16 September 2026, nine state a price, and they run from a $39-a-month lease to $24,000 paid over twelve months. The pages you get are a small part of the gap. The rest is whether you own the site when you stop paying, what is bundled with it — hosting and edits, a Business Profile, SEO, ad management — and the term: a 90-day minimum, month to month, twelve monthly payments or a one-time price.

Is a cheap plumber website a bad idea?

Not because it is cheap. The $39-a-month and $49-a-month options read on 16 September 2026 are leases or platform subscriptions: the site exists while you pay, and one platform says in its own words that the code cannot be moved to another host. That is fine for a first year if you know it, and a trap if you thought you were buying something. The question to ask is not the price but what happens on the day you stop paying.

What should a plumber check before paying for a website?

Four things, in writing: who owns the site, the domain and the content when you stop paying; what the monthly figure includes and what is a separate line; the term, the notice period and whether it renews; and how many pages you are getting. Four of the nine seller pages read on 16 September 2026 answer the first question on the page; five leave it for the call.
