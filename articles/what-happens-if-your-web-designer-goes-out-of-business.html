If your web designer goes out of business, you keep your website only if you already hold seven things.

The site does not vanish on the day the shop that built it does. What vanishes is the person who knew the passwords. Here is the exact list a handover must leave in your hands, how to check each item today, and what to do this week if the designer is already gone.

By Matthew Kerr Published 15 Sep 2026 Updated 15 Sep 2026 9 min read
Quick answer

A website is a domain, a folder of files, a hosting account and a handful of third-party accounts. If each is registered in your name with a login you hold, a designer closing changes nothing except who you call for edits.

Anything that sits under the designer’s account is the piece you can lose. The domain and the files are the two that are hard to get back; everything else can be recreated.

Our handover leaves all seven items in your name at launch — code, content, domain and accounts — and, for a static site, a folder you can host for free without us.

Nothing happens to a website on the day its designer closes. The server does not know the shop stopped answering email. The site keeps serving pages until a renewal goes unpaid, or until something breaks and nobody alive has the login to fix it. That gap between “closed” and “gone dark” is your window, and how wide it is depends entirely on what you were handed at launch.

What actually goes wrong when a designer disappears

Page one of Google on this question is a set of small-agency posts, and they agree about the failure. It is never the code. NorthMac Services puts it plainly: “When they vanish, those credentials vanish with them. You own your business name, your brand, your content, but you may not technically control the infrastructure.” (NorthMac Services, What Happens When Your Web Designer Disappears?, 28 April 2026, read 15 September 2026.) The same post lists what the designer typically holds under their own credentials: the domain registration, the hosting account and sometimes the email, and it recommends copies of all site files and database backups stored under your control, with renewal dates on payment methods in your name.

Marketing Clarity says the same thing from the other direction: “The business owner should still retain control” of the domain, the hosting and the site admin login even while a designer is doing the work, and it names the accounts most often set up under the designer’s name — registrar, hosting, WordPress, email and the Google Business Profile. (Marketing Clarity, What Happens If My Web Designer Disappears?, read 15 September 2026.) A UK agency podcast on the same question opens with: “Make sure you have access to your domain name registrar (the company that manages your domain).” (Clicking Mad, What should you do if your website designer goes bust?, 15 October 2024, read 15 September 2026.)

Every one of those posts is right, and every one of them stops at “make sure you have access.” None of them gives you the list. Here it is.

What to ask for, item by item

1. The domain

The ask, in writing: “Please confirm the domain is registered with me as the registrant, at a registrar where I hold the login, with my card on the renewal.” Netlify’s own domain documentation explains why the registrar matters more than the host: “When you register a domain, you’re essentially renting that web address from a domain registrar, which is a company authorized to sell domain registrations.” (Netlify Docs, Custom domains, read 15 September 2026.) The rent has to keep being paid by someone, and that someone should be you.

If the domain is under the designer’s account, ask for a transfer to a registrar of your own. That needs two things from them: the domain unlocked, and its authorization code. Cloudflare’s transfer guide: “Request an authorization code for your domain. This is also called an auth code, EPP code, authinfo code, or transfer code.” (Cloudflare Docs, Transfer your domain to Cloudflare, read 15 September 2026.) The same page says a transfer can take up to 10 days depending on the registrar, and that the domain must be at least 60 days past registration and not transferred in the last 60 days. GoDaddy’s transfer-away page adds a detail worth knowing before you change anything: “Domains can’t be transferred within 60 days of new registration, transfer, or contact info change if the current registrant opted to apply a 60–day lock.” It also has you turn off domain privacy and remove domain protection before the code is issued, and puts a typical transfer at five to seven days. (GoDaddy Help, Transfer my domain away from GoDaddy, read 15 September 2026.)

2. The hosting account, or the folder

The ask: “Is the hosting account in my name, and does the invoice come to me? If not, what do I get, and how fast, if you close?” A designer hosting your site as a service is normal — it is what our Care Plan is — but the answer to the second question has to be a folder, not a shrug. For a site with no database, that folder is the whole site. GitHub’s description of its own free tier is the clearest statement of what “static” means: “GitHub Pages is a static site hosting service that takes HTML, CSS, and JavaScript files straight from a repository on GitHub.” (GitHub Docs, What is GitHub Pages?, read 15 September 2026.) Files in, website out, nobody in the middle.

3. The source files

The ask: “Send me a zip of the site as it is today, and again after any major change.” Then open it. An older post that still sits on page one for this question, from 2008, gets the check exactly right: “You need to have access to all your files before your website designer disappears,” and it tells you to ask for the original editable graphic files, not just the published versions. (Passion for Business, Has Your Website Designer Disappeared?, 4 December 2008, read 15 September 2026.) For a CMS site the zip is not enough on its own; you also want the database export, and a list of any paid plugins licensed to the designer rather than to you, which Marketing Clarity flags as one of the things owners discover too late.

4. The content as files

The ask: “Send me the page copy as a document and the originals of every photo and the logo.” The live site is not a backup of the content; it is a rendering of it. If a rebuild is ever needed, the copy and the images are most of the work, and having them as files turns weeks into days.

5. The third-party accounts

The ask: “Create each account under my email and add yourself as a manager.” That covers Google Analytics, Search Console, the Google Business Profile, any ad account and every social page. The Business Profile is the one to check first, because your reviews live in it, and it is the account Marketing Clarity specifically names as commonly set up under the designer.

6. The DNS and email records

The ask: “Give me a one-page list of every DNS record on the domain and tell me where my email is hosted.” Pointing a domain at a new host is a small edit — GitHub’s custom-domain guide, for example, uses a CNAME record for a subdomain and A, ALIAS or ANAME records for the bare domain, and recommends verifying the domain first “in order to improve security and avoid takeover attacks.” (GitHub Docs, About custom domains and GitHub Pages, read 15 September 2026.) The edit is only small if you know which of the other records must not be touched. The mail records are the ones people delete by accident.

7. The termination clause

The ask: “Show me the clause.” Clicking Mad’s advice is to review your contract for what rights you have regarding the design and content of your site; Passion for Business asks for “written confirmation that you own the content of the website and have the right to transfer it, edit it.” If the agreement does not say what you get when the relationship ends, assume the answer is nothing and get it added.

The one email to send while they still answer

“Before we go further, I want the handover in hand: the domain registered to me at a registrar I log in to, a zip of the site files and the page copy and images, my email as owner on Analytics, Search Console and the Business Profile with you as manager, a list of the DNS records, and the clause in our agreement that says what I get if you close. Can you send those this week?”

A builder who intends to be around says yes. A builder who cannot say yes has just told you which items you do not hold.

What to do this week if your designer has already vanished

Order matters here. The site is still up; the clock is the next renewal.

  1. Today: save a copy of the live site while it is still live. Every page, every image, from the browser if that is all you have. NorthMac’s advice is to get copies of all site files and database backups under your control; if you have any login at all, export from there first.
  2. Today: find out where the domain lives. The registrar’s name is in the domain’s public registration record. If you can log in, check the registrant details and the renewal date. If the account is the designer’s, NorthMac’s route is the one that works: recover ownership through the registrar’s own verification procedure using your business documentation.
  3. Day two: if they still answer email, ask for exactly two things. Unlock the domain and send the authorization code. Then start the transfer to a registrar in your name. Budget five to ten days for it to complete, and remember the 60-day rule above before you change any registrant contact details.
  4. Day two: put your own card on every renewal you can reach. Domain and hosting. This is the difference between a site that stays up while you sort the rest out and one that goes dark on a date you did not know about.
  5. Day three: take the hosting export. If you have the host login, download the backup. If the site is static and you hold the files, you can put them on a static host and point the domain at it with the DNS records described above; a free static host charges nothing monthly for that.
  6. Day three: change every password you can. Clicking Mad: “Change all passwords: Particularly the ones for your website administration, hosting account, and domain registrar.”
  7. Day four: the accounts. Log in to the Business Profile, Analytics, Search Console and any ad account. Make sure your email is the owner and remove anyone who should no longer be there.
  8. Day five: decide rescue or rebuild. If you hold the domain and the files, any developer can put the site back up. If you hold the domain and nothing else, it is a rebuild: a Starter Site with us is from $999 (or $149/mo for 12 months, after which the site is yours; if it is never delivered, every payment is refunded). A Business Site is from $2,499. If you hold the files but do not know what state they are in, a Site & Business Audit is $750 and tells you before you spend more.
If you hold neither the domain nor the files

The registrar’s recovery process is the whole game, because the domain is the only item on the list that cannot be recreated. A site can always be rebuilt; an address that every customer, listing and review points at cannot. Start there, today, and treat everything else as recoverable.

Common questions

Does my web designer own my website?

In practice, whoever holds the logins controls the site, whatever the contract says. If the domain is registered in your name at a registrar you can log into, the hosting invoice comes to you, and you hold a copy of the files, the designer owns nothing you need. If any of those sit under the designer's account, read your agreement for the ownership and termination clauses and ask for the missing item in writing now, while they still answer email. Our project agreement says you own all of it at the end: code, content, domain and accounts, with no lock-in.

What happens to my domain if my web designer goes out of business?

If the domain is registered to you at a registrar where you hold the login and your card pays the renewal, nothing changes. If it was registered under the designer's account, you need the domain unlocked and its authorization code (also called an auth, EPP or transfer code) so you can move it to a registrar of your own; registrars say that takes roughly five to ten days and cannot happen within 60 days of a new registration, a transfer or a registrant change. If the designer cannot be reached, use the registrar's ownership-recovery process with your business documents.

Can I move my website to a new host without my designer?

Yes, if you hold two things: the site files (a static export, or a CMS backup with its database) and the login to wherever the domain's DNS records live. You upload the files to a new host and point the domain at it with a CNAME record for a subdomain or an A/ALIAS record for the bare domain. A static site can be hosted on a free static host with no monthly fee. If you have the domain but not the files, the site has to be rebuilt; a Starter Site with us is from $999.

What should a website handover include?

Seven things, each in your name with a login you hold: the domain, the hosting account or a static export of the site, the source files, the copy and images as files, the third-party accounts (analytics, Search Console, Google Business Profile, ads and social) with the designer as a manager rather than the owner, a written list of the DNS and email records, and a written statement of what happens if the relationship ends. If any one of those is missing, that is the piece you can lose when the designer closes.
