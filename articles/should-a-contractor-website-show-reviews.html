Should a contractor website show reviews? Yes — 27 of 39 Grand Rapids winners do, nine of them through a live widget

Every review-software vendor says yes and sells the widget, and page one for this question is their lists of review sites. I wanted the local number instead: of the trade sites that already hold the top map spots in Grand Rapids, how many show their reviews on the site, and how. On 16 September 2026 I read all 39 home pages and counted.

By Matthew Kerr Published 16 Sep 2026 Updated 16 Sep 2026 9 min read
Quick answer

Yes — and show the ones you already have rather than collecting new ones for the site. On 16 September 2026 I read the home pages of the 39 top-ranked Grand Rapids plumbers, roofers, HVAC and electrical companies (the 40 businesses from our 15 September map-pack sweep; one did not fetch). 27 show reviews on the home page itself: 9 through a live widget that pulls in Google reviews (Trustindex on five sites, Elfsight, NiceJob, Podium and Rize on one each), 10 print a star rating of 4.5 to 5.0, 9 print a count from 37 to 1,283, and 21 carry testimonial text. Six more only link out to their Google reviews. Six show nothing at all.

Two rules decide how. Google’s structured-data guideline, read 16 September 2026, says that when a business controls the reviews about itself, its pages using LocalBusiness markup are ineligible for the star feature — and it names an embedded Google reviews widget as the example. Eight of the 39 mark up a rating anyway. The FTC’s rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, announced 14 August 2024, bars fake or paid reviews, undisclosed insider testimonials, and presenting a filtered selection on your own site as all or most of what was submitted.

So: a widget or a plain rating and count pulled from Google, a link to read all of them there, quotes only with a real name and permission, and no star schema on your own pages. That is how our Business Site ($2,499 starting) is built — local schema, reviews and analytics are on the card — and our Managed Local plan handles the review requests and responses that make the number worth showing.

The honest version of this question is not whether reviews matter — 40 of 40 top-ranked Grand Rapids trades have a website and the twelve top map spots we read on 15 September held 22 to 12,142 reviews — but whether the reviews belong on the website too, and in what form. That is a question about what the site is for. The reviews already exist on Google; the site’s job is to let a visitor see them without leaving, and to do it in a way that does not break Google’s rules or the FTC’s. This page counts how the sites that already win do it, then reads the two rules.

The measurement: 39 home pages, five ways to show a review

On 16 September 2026 I fetched the home page of each of the 40 businesses in our 15 September sweep — the top ten map-pack results for plumber, roofer, HVAC and electrician in Grand Rapids — with curl, the same way a crawler does, and read the HTML for five signals: a third-party review widget script or class (Trustindex, Elfsight, NiceJob, Podium, Rize and the like), a printed star figure next to the word stars, rating or reviews, a printed review count, testimonial copy (a testimonials heading or a “what our customers say” section), and an AggregateRating or Review object in the page’s JSON-LD. I also recorded whether the page links out to its Google reviews. One electrical site did not answer, so the count is 39. The raw file is in the receipts.

What Google says about stars on your own site

Eight of the 39 home pages carry an AggregateRating or Review object in their structured data — Ferrer Plumbing, Roto-Rooter, Above Roofing, Mr. Roof, R&R Heating and Cooling, Pro-Tech, Vredevoogd and Amp Electric — presumably in the hope of star ratings under their listing in Google’s results. Google’s review-snippet documentation, read 16 September 2026, is direct about it: “If the entity that’s being reviewed controls the reviews about itself, their pages that use LocalBusiness or any other type of Organization structured data are ineligible for star review feature. For example, a review about entity A is placed on the website of entity A, either directly in their structured data or through an embedded third-party widget (for example, Google Business reviews or Facebook reviews widget).” The same page adds: “Ratings must be sourced directly from users. Don’t rely on human editors to create, curate, or compile ratings information for local businesses.”

So the markup on those eight pages is not doing what its owners think. It does no harm I can measure — 16 of 39 top-ranked sites rank without any business type in their schema — but it is not why anyone on this list is in the map pack. The stars a local business earns show on its Business Profile, in Maps and in the local results, and they come from the profile’s reviews, not from a snippet on the home page.

What the FTC rule says about testimonials on your site

Twenty-one of the 39 pages carry testimonial text — a quotes section, a “what our customers say” block, a page of praise. That is the oldest way to do it and it is still legal, with conditions the FTC wrote down. Its trade regulation rule on the use of consumer reviews and testimonials, announced 14 August 2024 and read on the FTC’s own site on 16 September 2026, prohibits “reviews and testimonials that misrepresent that they are by someone who does not exist, such as AI-generated fake reviews, or who did not have actual experience with the business”; prohibits “providing compensation or other incentives conditioned on the writing of consumer reviews expressing a particular sentiment”; prohibits testimonials by “officers or managers” that do not disclose the connection; and — the line that matters for a website — “bars a business from misrepresenting that the reviews on a review portion of its website represent all or most of the reviews submitted when reviews have been suppressed based upon their ratings or negative sentiment.” The rule lets the agency seek civil penalties; the vote was 5–0.

Google’s Business Profile help says the same thing about incentives from its side, read 16 September 2026: “Offering incentives, like free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for customers to post reviews, change reviews, or remove negative reviews is considered fake & misleading content and is strictly prohibited.” Its advice for getting more of them is plain: “To leave reviews, you can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code.”

Read together: a hand-picked quotes section is fine if the people are real, said it, and the section does not claim to be “our reviews” while the two-star ones were left out. A live widget that shows what Google shows sidesteps the question entirely, which is one reason nine of the top forty use one. None of this is legal advice; it is what the two documents say, with the dates I read them.

What this reading does not prove

  • Home pages only. A site with a separate reviews page and nothing on the home page counts as nothing here.
  • It is a read of HTML, not of screens. Pages drawn by JavaScript after load — at least two of the six “nothing” sites look that way — can show reviews this method cannot see.
  • Correlation, not cause. These 39 sites rank because of their profiles, distance and prominence; showing reviews on the site is what winners do, not what made them win.
  • The star figures are as printed. I read 4.7, 4.8, 4.9 off the pages; I did not check them against each profile.
  • The law is summarised from the FTC’s press release, not the rule text, and I am not a lawyer.

Common questions

Should a contractor put customer reviews on their website?

Yes. Of the 39 top-ranked Grand Rapids plumbers, roofers, HVAC and electrical home pages read on 16 September 2026, 27 show reviews on the page — 9 through a live widget, 10 with a printed star rating, 9 with a printed count from 37 to 1,283, 21 with testimonial text — and 6 more link out to their Google reviews. Only 6 show nothing. The simplest honest version is a rating and count pulled from your Google listing with a link to read them all there.

Do reviews on my own website help me rank on Google?

Not through the site. Google’s review-snippet guideline, read 16 September 2026, says that when a business controls the reviews about itself, its pages using LocalBusiness structured data are ineligible for the star feature, and it names an embedded Google reviews widget as an example; 8 of the 39 top-ranked sites mark up a rating anyway. The stars and the map-pack position come from the reviews on the Business Profile itself. Show reviews on the site for the visitor who is already there, not for the ranking.

Can I pick which reviews to show on my website?

You can choose quotes, but the FTC’s rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (announced 14 August 2024, read 16 September 2026) bars a business from presenting the reviews on a review section of its website as all or most of those submitted when negative ones have been suppressed, bars fake or incentivised reviews, and requires insiders to disclose their connection. A live widget showing what Google shows avoids the question; a quotes section is fine with real names, permission and no claim that it is the whole picture.
