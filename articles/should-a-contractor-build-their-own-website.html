Should a contractor build their own website? Yes, if you will finish it — 6 of 39 top-ranked Grand Rapids trades are on a DIY builder

Page one for this question is web designers explaining why you should not, and builder companies explaining why you should. Nobody checked what the contractors who actually rank in Grand Rapids built their sites on, so on 16 September 2026 I read all forty.

By Matthew Kerr Published 16 Sep 2026 Updated 16 Sep 2026 10 min read
Quick answer

Yes, you can, and a site you built yourself can sit in the map pack. On 16 September 2026 I read the platform behind each of the 40 Grand Rapids trade businesses in the map-pack lists from the 15 September sweep. Of the 39 that answered, six are on a do-it-yourself builder — Wix (3), Squarespace (2) and GoDaddy’s builder (1). The other 33 are on WordPress (18), an agency-hosted builder (5), a franchise brand’s platform (3) or custom code (7).

The map pack does not rank the platform; it ranks the Business Profile, its reviews and how close you are to the searcher. What the six DIY sites show is what a finished builder site needs and what an unfinished one skips: three of the six have a tap-to-call link, two have a form, one links a service page, none has a privacy page. Across all 39 the counts are 32, 26, 30 and 23.

So build it yourself if you have the evenings, someone to write the service pages and the patience to keep it current. Hire it out when it has been “almost done” for a month. A Starter Site here is $999 once for up to three pages, live in days, or $149 a month for twelve months with hosting, updates and edits included and the site yours at month twelve.

Search the question and you get two answers, each sold by the person giving it. Designers say a builder site looks like a builder site and will cost you calls. Builder companies say anyone can launch in an afternoon. Both skip the only fact that settles it for a Grand Rapids contractor: what the businesses already ranking here built on, and whether the ones who did it themselves are missing anything. So this page reads the forty, names the six, and then says who should build their own and who should not.

Every one of those four things is a menu item in the builder these sites are on. A tap-to-call link is a phone number typed into a button; a form is a drag; a privacy page is a template. The six are not missing them because the builder cannot do them. They are missing them because the person building the site stopped before that part — which is the honest risk of doing it yourself, and the one the designers’ blogs are gesturing at when they say “it will look like a builder site”. It is not the look. It is the stopping.

The builder is not what a DIY site lacks. Finishing is.

What the platform does and does not decide

  • It does not decide the map pack. These six rank beside 33 sites built every other way. What the top of the pack has in common is on the reviews page (the twelve top-three spots hold 22 to 12,142 reviews) and the measured checklist. The profile does the ranking; the site takes the call.
  • It does decide what you pay and in what shape. A builder is a subscription. The plans this site read on 16 September 2026 for the monthly-cost page run $17 to $37 a month, and they stop the day you stop paying. A built site is a one-time price and then hosting, which for a static site can be close to free.
  • It does decide whether the site can leave. On a builder the pages live on the builder’s platform. One plumber-website platform I read on 16 September 2026 says it plainly: “You cannot build the website with Webtricity and then move the code to another website hosting company” (webtricity.com/plumbing-websites, read 16 September 2026). The domain is the part you can always keep, if it is registered in your own name — how to check yours.
  • It does decide speed, a little. The DIY home pages in the sweep served between 69 kB and 1,089 kB of HTML. Size is not speed, but the speed measurement on ten Grand Rapids contractor sites found six taking more than 13 seconds to show their main content, and a builder theme with every widget switched on is one way to get there.

If you do build it yourself: the four things the six skipped

  1. A tap-to-call link. Three of the six DIY sites have one; 32 of 39 overall do. It is an href="tel:" on the number, and every builder has a phone-button block. How the links work and where to put them.
  2. A form that goes somewhere you read. Two of six. The builder’s form block is fine; the failure mode is the notification email going to an address nobody opens.
  3. One page per service you want to be found for. One of six links any. It does not have to be long; it has to say the service, the towns and the number.
  4. A privacy page. None of the six. If the site has a form or a pixel, it needs one, and the builder ships a template for it.

What this reading does not prove

  • Platform is read from fingerprints, not from the owner. A Wix site on its own domain reads as Wix; a WordPress site built by an agency reads as WordPress and is counted there, not under “agency-hosted”. Bergsma Plumbing’s page carried only three wp-content references and is counted as WordPress on that basis. If a business tells me its platform is misread, the table changes.
  • Served HTML only. Anything a page adds with JavaScript after load is invisible to this sweep, on the platform read and on the 15 September counts alike.
  • One site is missing. allaspectselectric.com timed out on 15 September and again on 16 September, so the electricians are nine of ten.
  • Correlation, not cause. Six DIY sites in the pack does not mean a DIY site gets you into the pack; it means it does not keep you out. The 15 September reviews reading is where the ranking lives.
  • No site is being graded. Every cell is what a named page did or did not serve on a stated date, and the raw file is linked so you can re-run it.

Common questions

Can a contractor build their own website and still rank?

Yes. Of the 39 top-ranked Grand Rapids trade sites whose platform could be read on 16 September 2026, six are on a do-it-yourself builder: three on Wix, two on Squarespace and one on GoDaddy’s builder. They sit in the same map-pack lists as the 33 built on WordPress, an agency-hosted builder, a franchise platform or custom code. The map pack ranks the Google Business Profile, its reviews and its distance from the searcher; the platform the site was built on is not what put them there.

What does a DIY contractor website usually lack?

The things that turn a visit into a call. Of the six DIY sites in the sweep, three have a tap-to-call link, two have a form, one links any service page and none links a privacy page. Across all 39 sites the counts are 32, 26, 30 and 23. A builder gives you the tools for all four; the sites that skip them are usually the ones nobody finished.
