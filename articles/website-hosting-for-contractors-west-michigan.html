Website hosting for contractors: I checked where 266 West Michigan trade sites live, and 30 WordPress sites were slow

Page one for “website hosting for contractors”, read on 10 October 2026, is eight pages from hosting companies, agencies and bloggers selling to contractors, one forum thread from an owner asking the same question, and a directory page. None of it looks at where contractors in a real local market actually host. So I looked at the servers behind 266 of them.

By Matthew Kerr Published 10 Oct 2026 Updated 10 Oct 2026 10 min read
Quick answer

Most West Michigan contractors never chose a host. On 10 October 2026 I looked up the servers behind the 266 working trade websites I read last month. 94 (35%) sit on a hosted builder or an agency’s platform such as Wix (30), Duda (16) or Squarespace (11), where hosting is part of the plan. 130 (49%) run WordPress, which does need a host, and I could put a name to the host of 82 of them: 19 different companies, with WP Engine the biggest at 23. 25 sit behind Cloudflare and do not say who hosts them, and 23 sit on a cloud server with no host named. The other 42 are custom builds, other systems or sites I could not identify.

Speed is where the host showed. I timed the first byte of each homepage from my Mac in Grand Rapids, then timed every slow one three more times. Sites on hosted builders stayed in a tight band: 1 of 94 was still slower than the 0.8 seconds web.dev calls good. WordPress sites were at both ends: 30 of 128 were still over 0.8 seconds after four fetches, and 8 were over the 1.8 seconds web.dev calls poor, while the quickest site in the whole reading was a WordPress one too. Of the 42 WordPress sites whose response names WP Engine, Flywheel, Pantheon, Kinsta or Automattic, 1 was slow. Of the 40 on other named hosting companies, 17 were.

So if you are on a builder, your hosting question is really an exit question: read who owns your website and domain. If you are on WordPress, the host is a choice you can test, and the questions to ask are below. Hosting is the part of a website nobody sees until it breaks. It is the computer that holds your pages and sends them to a customer’s phone. Usually it is one of two arrangements: a builder rents you the whole thing, or you, or whoever built the site, rent a server and put WordPress or a custom site on it. Which one the local competition uses, and with whom, is something I could read from public information, so I did.

How I measured it

The sites are the 266 trade homepages from my 23 September 2026 platform reading: the roofers, plumbers, heating and cooling companies, electricians, landscapers, painters and remodelers Brave Search lists in West Michigan towns, one row per website address. Dentists are left out, because they have their own page.

Between 3:07 and 3:13 a.m. Eastern on 10 October 2026 I did four things for each site. I asked public DNS which address the site’s name points to. I asked Team Cymru’s address lookup, a public service, which company’s network owns that address. I fetched the homepage once with curl, the way a browser would, and kept the response headers. And I timed how long the first byte took to arrive. That was one request per site with no retries, apart from the slow ones, which I come back to.

A network is not always the host, so each WordPress and custom site got its name from the first rule that fit:

  1. A header with a host’s name in it. WP Engine’s servers answer with a header that says so; Flywheel, Pantheon and Kinsta each leave their own mark, and so do Automattic’s servers, the company that runs WordPress.com.
  2. The network that answers, when it belongs to a hosting company such as Liquid Web, Hostinger or A2 Hosting.
  3. Neither, because the address belongs to a big cloud or to Cloudflare. I say that and do not guess. Cloudflare usually sits in front of a host rather than being the host, and it hides which one.

How fast they answer

The number I timed is the first byte: how long from the request until the first piece of the page arrives, counting any redirect, the connection and the security handshake. web.dev, Google’s web guidance site, calls 0.8 seconds or less good and anything over 1.8 seconds poor (web.dev/articles/ttfb, read 10 October 2026). It is not how long the whole page takes to appear; it is the part that the host controls most.

A single reply can be slow because nobody had asked for that page in a while. So every site that took over 0.8 seconds on the first fetch, 69 of 262, I fetched three more times, 10 seconds apart. For 36 of them the median of the four fetches was 0.8 seconds or less: that is the cold-cache effect, where the first visitor waits and the next ones do not. The other 33 stayed slow. The middle is the same everywhere. The median first byte was 0.50 seconds for hosted builders, 0.46 for WordPress and 0.49 for the rest, so a typical site is fine whatever it runs on. The difference is in the tail. Hosted builders had 1 slow site in 94, and the slowest took 1.6 seconds on the first fetch. WordPress had 30 in 128, and the slowest took 6.3 seconds. The quickest site in the reading, at 0.14 seconds, was WordPress as well. WordPress is where a good host and a poor one show up side by side.

Within WordPress, the sites whose response names WP Engine, Flywheel, Pantheon, Kinsta or Automattic were almost never slow (1 of 42), and those on the other named hosting companies often were (17 of 40). I am not going to rank hosting companies from groups of two to seven sites, and I do not know which plan any of these owners pays for: a cheap plan and a dear plan at the same company can behave very differently. The pattern is a reason to test, not a verdict on any brand.

One more reason not to wave the first-fetch figure away. A small local site is exactly the kind that has gone quiet when a customer arrives, so the first fetch is what that customer gets. 69 of 262 sites (26%) were over 0.8 seconds on it. For the speed of the whole page, which this reading does not measure, see whether speed matters for a local business site.

A Michigan footnote

9 of the 266 sites answer from Liquid Web’s network (6 of them WordPress). Liquid Web’s own page says Lansing, Michigan is its founding location and that it runs two data centers there (liquidweb.com/data-centers/lansing-web-hosting, read 10 October 2026). That does not put those 9 sites in Lansing: an address does not tell me which of a company’s data centers a site is in. It is only a note that one of the names in the list has a Michigan home.

What this reading does not prove

  • The network is not the invoice. A hosting company can resell another’s servers, and Cloudflare can sit in front of anything. The names are who answers, not always who you would pay.
  • One place, one night. The timings are from one Mac in Grand Rapids between 3:07 and 3:13 a.m. Eastern. At a busy hour, or from another city, they would differ.
  • First byte, not the whole page. A fast first byte can still lead to a heavy page. This is the part the host controls most, not all of it.
  • Small groups. The largest named host has 23 sites and most have under seven, so I do not rank companies.
  • Plans differ. I cannot see whether a site is on a company’s cheapest plan or its dearest.
  • Correlation, not cause. An owner on one of the five hosts named in that first group may also have had a better site built.
  • Four sites did not answer. One returned 401, one 403, one timed out, and one served a certificate chain curl could not verify (browsers sometimes repair those, so I flag it rather than call it broken). The other 262 answered over HTTPS with a certificate that verified.

Common questions

Where do most contractors host their website?

There is no single place. Of 266 West Michigan trade websites read on 10 October 2026, 94 (35%) sit on a hosted builder or an agency’s platform such as Wix (30), Duda (16) or Squarespace (11), where hosting comes with the plan. 130 (49%) run WordPress on a separate host: 19 hosting companies could be named, WP Engine most often with 23, and 25 sat behind Cloudflare without showing who hosts them.

Does the hosting company change how fast a contractor’s site loads?

On WordPress it did, in this reading. After four fetches, 30 of 128 WordPress sites still took longer than 0.8 seconds to the first byte, the line web.dev calls good, against 1 of 94 sites on hosted builders. Of the 42 WordPress sites whose response names WP Engine, Flywheel, Pantheon, Kinsta or Automattic, 1 was slow; of the 40 on other named hosting companies, 17 were. One place and one night, so test your own site before you decide anything.
