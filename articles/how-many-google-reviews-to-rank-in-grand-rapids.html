The question comes up in almost every first conversation I have with a trade owner: how many reviews do I need before Google puts me in the map pack? Page one of Google answers it with national medians. One 2026 study I read while writing this puts the median at 58 reviews across 453 ranked businesses and the range at “1 review to 4,400” (Jerod Johnson, getfoundguy.com, 3 June 2026, read 15 September 2026). Another post says flatly, “There is no magic review number.” (Vick Antonyan, humblehelp.studio, 28 June 2026, read 15 September 2026). Both are right, and neither tells you what it takes on 28th Street.

So I measured it.

What Google says decides it

Google publishes a short page on this and it is more useful than most of what is written about it. “Local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity,” it says, and then explains the three under the headings Relevance, Distance and Prominence. Relevance, in Google’s words, is how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for. Distance is how far each business is from the customer who is searching. Prominence “means how well-known a business is,” and the page says that factor is also based on how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have, adding: “More reviews and positive ratings can help your business’s local ranking.” It also says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. (Google Business Profile Help, Tips to improve your local ranking on Google, read 15 September 2026.)

So reviews live inside one of three factors, and the other two are visible in my tables. Distance alone means my list from one spot in Grand Rapids is not your customer’s list from theirs, and it is a plausible reason a 22-review roofer can list above a 437-review one. Relevance is the likeliest explanation for an HVAC company surfacing for electrician: Maps matched what that profile says it does to the words I typed. I cannot see inside Google’s system, so I will call both of those plausible and no more. What the reading itself supports is narrower and firmer: in this city, review count is one input among several, and rating is the one nobody in the top three has skipped.

What this means for you

Take the reading literally and it gives you an order of operations.

First, the rating. Every top-three business is at 4.4 or above, and nine of the twelve are at 4.8 or above. If you are sitting at 4.1 with 90 reviews, more asking is not your first problem; the next twenty reviews need to be good ones, which means fixing whatever the bad ones keep naming. Google’s page puts reviews and positive ratings in the same sentence. The ratings half is not optional.

Second, a first target you can actually see. The smallest review count that held a top-three spot on 15 September was 224 for plumbers, 78 for roofers, 22 for HVAC and 35 for electricians. Those are not thresholds. A business at 30 HVAC reviews is guaranteed nothing. But they are the lowest counts that were sufficient for somebody, today, in this city, and they are a great deal more useful to you than a national median of 58.

Third, keep going, because the leader has hundreds. In three of the four trades the biggest count in the top three is in the hundreds or thousands: 2,286 for plumbing, 229 for roofing, 1,291 for HVAC. Electrical is the exception. The largest count among the three electricians in the top three is 115, and the only two listings above 200 in that whole top ten, Vredevoogd and Amp Electric Heating & Air, both carry heating and air in their names. Across all forty businesses listed, the median is 218. Reaching the smallest sufficient count gets you into the conversation; it does not make you the safe choice against a competitor with ten times your reviews at the same rating.

If you are already past both bars

If your rating is above 4.4, your count is above your trade’s smallest top-three number, and you are still not in the pack, the problem is probably not reviews. It is more likely your primary category, your address, how complete the profile is, or the words on it, which is the relevance and distance side of Google’s page. No amount of asking fixes that.

How to ask for reviews without breaking Google’s rules

Whatever count you settle on, the way you get there matters, because Google removes reviews it decides were manufactured and can restrict a profile over it. The rules are short and public.

  • Ask everyone, not just the happy ones. Google’s Maps content policy says merchants may not “Discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers.” That rules out the common funnel where customers rate you privately first and only the five-stars get sent on to Google, whichever software sells it.
  • No rewards. The same policy bars offering “incentives – such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services” for a review, and Google’s tips page calls that fake and misleading content. No gift card, no raffle, no ten percent off.
  • Make it one tap. Google’s own advice is to “ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code.” Put the link in the invoice email and the QR code on the paperwork you leave behind.
  • Do not stand over them. The policy says merchants should not pressure customers to leave reviews while on the premises, and should not ask for specific content. Ask at the end of the job and let them write it from their own couch.
  • Reply, including to the bad ones. Google’s tips page says to reply to reviews and that “A mix of positive and negative feedback often feels more trustworthy.” The top three in every trade I measured includes businesses with hundreds of reviews and an average below 5.0.

Sources: Prohibited & Restricted Content (Maps user-contributed content policy) and Tips to get more reviews (Google Business Profile Help), both read 15 September 2026.

How to decide

Three questions, in order.

  1. Is your rating 4.4 or higher? If not, stop reading about counts. Read what the low reviews say, fix the one that keeps recurring, and ask every customer for the next three months. Twelve out of twelve top-three businesses cleared this bar.
  2. Where does your count sit against your trade’s smallest sufficient number? That was 224 for plumbing, 78 for roofing, 22 for HVAC and 35 for electrical on 15 September. Below it, the job is a steady, honest asking habit and nothing fancier. Above it and still absent from the pack, spend your money on finding out why, not on more review software.

Does my star rating matter more than my review count?

In this reading, rating was the only thing all twelve top-three businesses had in common: none was below 4.4 and nine were at 4.8 or above, while review counts varied from 22 to 12,142. Google's help page says more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking, so both count. A practical order is rating first, then a steady count.

Can I offer a discount or gift card for a Google review?

No. Google's Maps content policy prohibits merchants from offering incentives such as payment, discounts or free goods in exchange for a review, and from discouraging negative reviews or selectively asking only happy customers. Google can remove the reviews and restrict the profile. Ask every customer, give them the link or QR code, and let the review be whatever it is.
